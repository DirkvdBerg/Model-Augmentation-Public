Fisher Information Matrix-Based Output Channel Weighting for Multi-Trajectory Closed-Loop Parameter RecoveryIntroduction and System FormulationThe identification of complex continuous-time quasi-Linear Parameter-Varying (LPV) mechanical systems presents a multifaceted challenge in computational mechanics and control theory. In systems characterized by position- or state-dependent dynamics—where state-space matrices $A(\rho(x,t))$ and $B(\rho(x,t))$ evolve continuously as functions of endogenous scheduling variables $\rho$—recovering underlying physical parameters such as mass, stiffness, and damping matrices requires sophisticated optimization architectures. The problem is heavily compounded when the data is derived from closed-loop trajectories characterized by active feedback control, resulting in heterogeneous excitation and intentionally dormant output channels.The specific architecture under analysis involves the recovery of 13 physical parameters belonging to a continuous-time quasi-LPV mechanical system. The system yields three continuous output channels ($X_1$, $X_2$, and $Y$, measured in meters). To satisfy physical constraints (e.g., preventing negative mass or stiffness), the optimization landscape employs log-space parameter reparameterization. The trajectories are formulated as three distinct experimental groups:Group A: A state where only $Y$ is subjected to excitation, while $X_1$ and $X_2$ are authoritatively suppressed to near-zero by a feedback controller.Group B: A state where $X_1$ and $X_2$ are actively excited, but $Y$ is held constant by the feedback regulator.Group C: A state of full dynamic coupling where all three channels are excited simultaneously.The numerical integration relies on a Runge-Kutta 4th order (RK4) scheme within a batched multiple-shooting framework. Multiple shooting mitigates the extreme sensitivity of long-horizon Initial Value Problems (IVPs) by partitioning the trajectory into discrete segments, treating the initial state of each segment as an optimization variable, and enforcing continuity via defect constraints. The objective function is driven by the Adam optimizer. However, a critical pathology emerges when applying a uniform Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss across these heterogeneous trajectories: the gradient-based optimizer will inevitably attempt to fit the closed-loop rejection dynamics and numerical integration artifacts of the dormant channels.The proposed solution to circumvent this pathology involves computing the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) at initialization to derive per-channel, per-trajectory scalar weights based on the output sensitivity norms ($s_c = \sum_t |\partial \hat{y}_c / \partial \theta|^2$). This report exhaustively analyzes the theoretical validity, practical mechanics, and optimization implications of this FIM-based loss weighting framework.1. The Fisher Information Matrix as a Loss Weighting MechanismThe Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) is the cornerstone of statistical parameter estimation, quantifying the expected amount of information that an observable random variable carries about an unknown parameter vector $\theta$. Within the domain of system identification and optimal control, the FIM has an extensive history, though its application must be strictly bifurcated into two distinct regimes: a priori experiment design and a posteriori parameter estimation weighting.FIM in Optimal Experiment DesignIn the classical literature, the FIM is predominantly utilized prior to data collection to synthesize optimal input trajectories. The core mathematical objective is to maximize the information content of the data, which is geometrically equivalent to minimizing the volume of the parameter confidence ellipsoid. This is formally known as model-based optimal experimental design. For instance, D-optimal design seeks to maximize the determinant of the FIM, $\det(FIM)$, thereby minimizing the generalized variance of the parameter estimates. A-optimal design focuses on minimizing the trace of the inverse FIM to reduce the average variance of the estimates, while E-optimal design maximizes the minimum eigenvalue of the FIM to improve the worst-case parameter uncertainty.In these contexts, the FIM serves as an a priori variance estimate and a summary of the output sensitivity functions, allowing control engineers to manipulate the input spectrum to ensure that the resulting experimental data will be sufficiently informative. If a system exhibits non-invertible or rank-deficient FIMs—often due to unidentifiable or heavily correlated parameters—methodologies such as pseudo-inverse (PI) approaches or leave-out (LO) parameter clustering are required to generate valid experimental designs.FIM for Loss Weighting in Parameter EstimationThe application of the FIM, or its derivative sensitivity metrics, directly as a weighting mechanism during parameter estimation from already-collected data is less universally standardized but possesses firm roots in frequency-weighted robust control and iterative estimation. In control-relevant system identification, parameter estimation algorithms frequently utilize pre- and post-frequency-dependent weighting matrices to shape the estimation error. This ensures the resulting model prioritizes the frequency bands most critical for closed-loop stability, such as setpoint tracking or disturbance rejection, effectively shifting the spectral radius of the error.In time-domain formulations, iterative weighted least squares (IRLS) methods approximate robust estimators by continuously regressing weights based on error contamination. Furthermore, in finite element modeling and spatial boundary problems, weighted least squares responses are explicitly engineered to scale the objective function locally, preventing the optimizer from attempting to capture sharp, physically impossible gradients that result in massive errors in dormant or stable domains.Explicitly flagged as unverified inference: While utilizing the FIM for input design and using generalized weighted least squares for error shaping are fully established in the literature, the specific extraction of the unscaled scalar trace of the per-channel sensitivity Jacobian ($s_c = \sum_t |\partial \hat{y}_c / \partial \theta|^2$) to serve directly as a fixed, positive proportionality weight in an MSE loss function is an innovative, heuristic bridge between these domains. Standard statistical maximum likelihood formulations weight the loss by the inverse noise covariance, not by the forward parameter sensitivity magnitude. However, mathematically, applying $s_c$ as a weight acts as a continuous attention mechanism. Closed-Loop Identification and the Dormant Channel PathologyThe recovery of parameters from closed-loop data represents one of the most perilous tasks in system identification. The fundamental assumption of open-loop identification—that the input $u(t)$ is uncorrelated with the measurement noise $v(t)$—is completely violated in closed-loop systems. Because the feedback controller computes the input based on the measured output, the input sequence becomes highly correlated with the noise and unmodeled disturbances present in the output.The Confounding Nature of Suppressed ChannelsWhen a system undergoes trajectory experiments where specific channels are intentionally suppressed (e.g., Groups A and B in the provided system description), the dynamics observed on these dormant channels are not reflective of the open-loop physical plant. Instead, the residual variations on $X_1$ and $X_2$ during a Group A trajectory consist entirely of sensor noise, minute actuator corrections, and integration artifacts resulting from the controller's active rejection of cross-coupled disturbances.If an unweighted MSE loss function is applied across all channels simultaneously, the optimizer is forced to minimize the discrepancy between the physical model and these closed-loop rejection artifacts. Published evidence heavily documents the catastrophic bias this induces. During the flight test system identification of the NASA X-56A Multi-Utility Technology Testbed (MUTT), a highly flexible aircraft flown under closed-loop control to suppress flutter, researchers documented severe parameter bias when attempting to estimate structural vibration parameters directly alongside aerodynamic stability derivatives. The optimization algorithms repeatedly confounded the structural terms with the active control inputs, leading to distorted, biased parameter estimates. Similarly, in rotorcraft and multi-input multi-output (MIMO) aircraft identification, the lack of sufficient independent excitation across all degrees of freedom forces parameter drift as the algorithms attempt to resolve the mathematically ill-posed mapping between suppressed outputs and physical states.Published Remedies and the Role of Binary MasksThe literature prescribes several remedies to combat closed-loop bias on suppressed channels. A primary mathematical remedy is data informativity analysis, which involves screening the data to ensure that specific conditions for parameter identifiability are met before optimization begins. If a channel lacks informative content, it must be excluded. In aerospace applications, this is practically achieved through decoupled maneuvers and the reduction of the output vector. By analyzing only the degrees of freedom that exhibit high coherence and excitation, researchers manually truncate the order of the system during specific identification windows, preventing the optimizer from fitting the noise of dormant states.In the domains of modern robotics and visual-motor control, this selective inclusion is operationalized via binary masks. When training models to predict grasp synthesis, object manipulation, or optical flow from heterogeneous data, binary masks are generated to explicitly zero out uninformative, dormant, or occluded regions of the state space. For instance, in UAV tracking and manipulation environments, paired complementary binary masks are utilized to strictly enforce which spatial or temporal channels are processed by the network's loss function, ensuring that the model does not update weights based on static or suppressed background states.However, binary masks require predefined, hard thresholds. The user must manually declare a channel "active" or "dormant." The proposed continuous FIM-based weight $s_c$ represents a highly elegant advancement over binary masks. Because $s_c$ evaluates the analytical Jacobian of the output with respect to the parameters, it inherently computes the exact limit of observability. If a feedback controller perfectly suppresses $X_1$, the parameter sensitivity on that channel approaches zero, and the weight autonomously scales the MSE loss for $X_1$ to zero without requiring manual binary truncation.The evidence regarding closed-loop identification bias, flight test parameter confounding, data informativity requirements, and the usage of binary masks in robotics discussed in this section draws from , and.3. Parameter Estimation in the Noiseless Simulation LimitEvaluating the FIM-based weighting framework requires confronting the edge-case of noiseless simulation data. The theoretical optimum for a weighted least squares or Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) dictates that the weighting matrix $W$ should be the inverse of the measurement noise covariance matrix, $W = \Sigma^{-1}$. This formulation normalizes each output channel by its statistical reliability. However, in a pristine, noiseless MATLAB simulation environment, the noise covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is the zero matrix, rendering $\Sigma^{-1}$ a degenerate, undefined singularity.Resolution of the Degenerate Weighting CaseAddressing parameter estimation in the absence of noise requires transitioning from statistical precision formulations to algebraic projection methodologies. The literature on optimal reduced-rank modeling and nullspace estimation provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for this regime. When defining a generalized weighting matrix for an estimation problem subject to noise, the optimal weighting matrix $W_{opt}$ is a complex function of the noise covariance. However, mathematical analysis establishes that as the noise variance approaches zero ($\sigma^2 \to 0$), the optimal weighting matrix fundamentally simplifies. The asymptotic limit dictates that the statistically optimal weight collapses strictly to the unweighted identity matrix, $W = I$.In this noiseless limit, the standard unweighted pseudo-inverse solution becomes a fully consistent estimator. It achieves perfect reconstruction because there is no stochastic variance to distort the underlying basis vectors of the system's nullspace. Furthermore, in studies utilizing synthetic, noiseless Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) simulation data for pharmacokinetic modeling, parameters are reliably estimated using direct, unweighted geometrical fitting, proving that inverse-variance weighting is not a prerequisite for convergence in the absence of noise.Sensitivity Norms as Algebraic ProjectorsIn the context of the proposed FIM weighting, substituting the undefined $\Sigma^{-1}$ with the sensitivity norm $s_c$ is highly appropriate for noiseless simulations. Because there is no noise floor to obscure minute parameter responses, the magnitude of $s_c$ purely reflects the algebraic coupling between the parameter $\theta$ and the output $\hat{y}_c$.Explicitly flagged as unverified inference: In a mathematically perfect simulation, if a parameter is structurally unobservable from a specific channel, the derivative $\partial \hat{y}_c / \partial \theta$ will be exactly zero, and $s_c$ will be exactly zero. Therefore, applying $s_c$ directly as a weight acts as a flawless algebraic projector, isolating the observable subspace. However, because fixed-step RK4 integrators and batched multiple shooting solvers introduce minute floating-point truncation errors and defect constraint tolerances, $s_c$ will practically evaluate to a microscopically small non-zero float. To prevent the Adam optimizer from amplifying these integration artifacts via the log-space reparameterization, it is highly recommended to normalize the $s_c$ values relative to the maximum channel sensitivity, or apply a soft-thresholding function before integrating them into the MSE loss.The analytical limits of estimation weighting in low-noise and noiseless regimes, along with the behavior of unweighted consistent estimators, discussed in this section draw from , and.4. Optimization Dynamics: Initialization versus Iterative ReweightingIntegrating FIM-based weights into an automatic differentiation pipeline trained by the Adam optimizer forces a critical structural decision: whether to compute the sensitivity weights once at the start of training and hold them fixed, or to continuously update them as the parameters evolve.The Adam Optimizer and Momentum ConsistencyThe Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) optimizer maintains two internal state variables for every parameter: an exponentially decaying moving average of past gradients (the first moment, or momentum) and an exponentially decaying moving average of past squared gradients (the second raw moment, or uncentered variance). These moving averages are the foundation of Adam's efficiency, allowing it to navigate complex loss landscapes by dampening oscillations and accelerating along consistent gradient directions.If the FIM-based channel weights $s_c$ are re-evaluated and altered iteratively during the training process, the fundamental topology of the MSE loss landscape shifts beneath the optimizer. When this occurs, the historical momentum stored in Adam's internal state becomes instantly uncorrelated with the new loss topography. The optimizer will apply a momentum vector derived from an outdated channel weighting scheme to the current, re-weighted gradients, resulting in severely sub-optimal steps, unstable oscillations, and potential divergence.The Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) ParadigmContinuously updating the weights in a loss function based on the current parameter estimates falls under the formal domain of Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) minimization. IRLS is heavily utilized in sparse recovery, robust statistics, and transient object identification. However, the literature is highly explicit regarding the combination of iterative reweighting algorithms and adaptive gradient descent methods.If an IRLS-style continuous update is required because the initial parameter guess is so poor that the initial FIM is invalid, standard practice mandates the strict resetting of optimizer states. In frameworks applying alternating Tweedie regression or IRLS updates within deep neural pipelines, the optimizer's gradients must be explicitly reset to zero (e.g., via .zero_grad()) within each epoch or update cycle.More severely, when deep learning architectures perform major structural updates—such as resetting opacities, modifying spatial sparsity masks, or reweighting loss functions based on evolving uncertainties—the optimization process must be temporarily paused. The Adam optimizer's momentum and variance buffers must be completely flushed, and the learning rate often requires a recalibration or "warm-up" phase to allow the reconstruction to stabilize against the newly defined objective function before resuming standard gradient descent.Recommendation derived from optimization literature: Computing the FIM weights once at initialization and keeping them fixed is the definitively superior approach to ensure Adam optimizer consistency, provided the initial parameter estimates are within the physically valid basin of attraction. If the quasi-LPV system requires IRLS because the initial sensitivities are wildly inaccurate, the reweighting must occur at sparse, discrete intervals (e.g., every 500 or 1000 epochs). Synergies with Optimal Input Design: Multisine ExcitationTo maximize parameter recovery accuracy in highly coupled, closed-loop mechanical systems, manipulating the loss function must be paired with optimal input design. Group A, B, and C trajectories require persistent excitation to ensure that the parameter space is fully explored. The utilization of multisine input signals is an established, highly robust mechanism for achieving this.FIM Determinant Maximization via MultisinesA multisine signal is a deterministic, periodic input comprised of a sum of sinusoids with specifically engineered frequencies, amplitudes, and orthogonal phase shifts. The literature intimately links multisine design to the Fisher Information Matrix. Generating an informative experiment is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the volume of the FIM, which is quantified by its determinant. By designing multisine inputs that specifically maximize the FIM determinant (D-optimal design) within the bandwidth of the system's dominant time constants, researchers can minimize the parameter covariance matrix.When applying multisine trajectories to complex mechanical models, such as bicycle rider control dynamics, optimizing the FIM via multisines allows for parameters to be consistently estimated with a relative error margin of less than 3%. Furthermore, employing "zippered" or "shifted" multisine designs ensures orthogonality across multi-channel inputs, dramatically reducing cross-correlation and enhancing the identifiability of off-diagonal parameters.Automatic Adaptation to Partial ExcitationThe most profound advantage of the proposed FIM-based loss weighting framework manifests when analyzing the interaction between multisine inputs and the controller-suppressed channels. In a quasi-LPV system, cross-channel mechanical coupling (e.g., off-diagonal stiffness or damping elements) means that energy injected into channel $Y$ will naturally attempt to propagate into $X_1$ and $X_2$. Even though the feedback controller actively suppresses $X_1$ and $X_2$, the aggressive, broadband frequencies of a multisine input may occasionally overwhelm the controller's bandwidth, resulting in transient, partial excitation on the nominally dormant channels.If rigid binary masks were employed, these transient partial excitations would be completely ignored, discarding highly valuable data regarding the cross-coupling parameters. However, because the proposed weighting relies on the analytical Jacobian ($\partial \hat{y} / \partial \theta$), it dynamically captures this reality. If a multisine on $Y$ forces a micro-response on $X_1$ that depends on the cross-stiffness parameter, the analytical sensitivity $s_{X1}$ will evaluate to a non-zero magnitude. The FIM-based weighting automatically adapts, assigning a small but proportional weight to $X_1$, thereby capturing the coupled physics without requiring the user to manually redefine the masking logic.The principles of multisine input design, D-optimal optimization, and the maximization of the Fisher Information Matrix determinant discussed in this section draw from , and.6. Stochastic Generalization: Extension to Noisy Measurement EnvironmentsWhile the primary validation environment utilizes a pristine MATLAB simulation, practical application on physical hardware requires addressing the presence of measurement noise. The transition from a deterministic framework to a stochastic one necessitates a rigorous integration of statistical variance into the sensitivity weighting.The Heteroscedastic FIM FormulationIn the presence of Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN), the Fisher Information Matrix is not merely a geometric construct of sensitivities; it is intrinsically modulated by the precision of the measurements. Formally, for an AWGN model, the elements of the FIM are defined as $I(\theta)_{i,j} = \frac{2}{\sigma^2_n} \Re(...) $, scaling the expected value of the squared gradients directly by the inverse of the noise variance. This principle establishes that the fundamental information utility of any output channel is inversely proportional to its stochastic noise power.Therefore, for real noisy measurements, the scalar sensitivity norm $s_c$ must be divided by the empirical or estimated variance of the noise on that specific channel, $\sigma^2_{\text{noise},c}$. The correctly formulated extended loss function becomes:$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_c \left( \frac{s_c}{\sigma^2_{\text{noise},c}} \right) \cdot \text{MSE}_c$$Corroboration in Multi-Output EstimationThis specific mathematical topology—a composite sensitivity-and-noise weighting—is heavily corroborated in the contemporary system identification and deep learning literature. Advanced estimation architectures, such as the CPA-Net framework developed for blind joint signal parameter estimation, explicitly employ a heteroscedastic loss function. These models utilize channel-wise attention mechanisms that modulate the contribution of each channel based on a combined evaluation of its predictive utility (sensitivity) and its predicted log-variance (noise). This adaptive, channel-wise weighting is recognized as particularly advantageous in heterogeneous hardware conditions where varying noise floors and channel impairments exist simultaneously.Crucially, this extended formulation strictly reduces to the standard multi-output Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) weighting. In classical MLE, the cost function minimizes the residuals weighted by the inverse of the noise covariance matrix $\Sigma^{-1}$. If we assume that the cross-channel noise correlation is zero (making $\Sigma$ a purely diagonal matrix composed of $\sigma^2_{\text{noise},c}$ entries), and we multiply the MSE cost by the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian $s_c$, we are functionally projecting the standard MLE cost onto the principal components of the parameter sensitivity space. The optimizer is mathematically compelled to balance the deterministic physical responsiveness of the channel against its stochastic unreliability, providing optimal parameter convergence.The statistical mechanics of Additive White Gaussian Noise, the derivation of heteroscedastic loss functions, and the reduction to standard Maximum Likelihood Estimators discussed in this section draw from , and.7. Applicability to Grey-Box Models and Neural AugmentationThe evolution of system identification increasingly leverages grey-box modeling, where explicit first-principles physics (such as the quasi-LPV equations of motion) are augmented with black-box neural networks. These neural networks introduce non-physical latent states and thousands of network weights ($\theta_{NN}$) designed to model complex residual dynamics, such as unmodeled aerodynamic drag or nonlinear Stribeck friction.FIM Viability and BackpropagationThe theoretical mechanics of the Fisher Information Matrix and output sensitivity extend seamlessly into the realm of neural-augmented models. Evaluating the FIM or its diagonal approximations for predictive densities parameterized by deep networks is a highly established practice. The Jacobian of the predicted output with respect to the parameters, $\partial \hat{y}(t,\theta) / \partial \theta$, propagates flawlessly through both the physical differential equations and the dense multi-layer perceptrons via the chain rule of reverse-mode automatic differentiation.Therefore, the output sensitivity norm $s_c$ will correctly capture the informative channels for a hybrid model. If a neural network is explicitly structured such that its latent state outputs feed only into the differential equation governing channel $Y$, the automatic differentiation engine will analytically calculate a high sensitivity magnitude for $Y$ concerning the network parameters $\theta_{NN}$, and an exact zero sensitivity for $X_1$ and $X_2$. The weighting framework scales perfectly.Overparameterization and Rank-DeficiencyHowever, a critical mathematical caveat must be addressed regarding overparameterization. By their nature, neural networks introduce massive parameter redundancy. When a model possesses more parameters than required to map the input-output relationship, the FIM becomes highly ill-conditioned, and its determinant approaches zero. While the scalar norm $s_c$ (representing the trace of the diagonal sensitivities) remains computable even if the full FIM is singular, the initialization of the neural network can corrupt the weighting schema.Explicitly flagged as unverified inference: In grey-box training, the neural network weights $\theta_{NN}$ are frequently initialized to near-zero distributions. This is done purposefully so that the model begins training exactly at the baseline physics prediction, slowly incorporating neural corrections over time. If the FIM weights $s_c$ are calculated at initialization using the full parameter vector $\theta = [\theta_{phys}, \theta_{NN}]$, the Jacobians with respect to the near-zero neural parameters will be artificially infinitesimal. This could incorrectly suppress the weight of a channel that heavily relies on unmodeled dynamics.To circumvent this, grey-box implementations should compute the FIM-based channel weight $s_c$ exclusively using the gradients of the physical parameters ($\partial \hat{y} / \partial \theta_{phys}$). By projecting the purely physics-based sensitivity onto the channels to define the global loss weights, the dormant channels are correctly masked, while the neural network parameters can still safely optimize within the physically-informed, actively weighted channels.The application of the Fisher Information Matrix to deep neural networks, the impact of overparameterization, and the mechanics of hybrid sensitivities discussed in this section draw from , and.8. Computational Tractability: Jacobian Evaluations in Modern AD FrameworksThe practical execution of the proposed solution requires computing the output sensitivity Jacobian $\partial \hat{y}(t,\theta) / \partial \theta$ across batched multiple-shooting trajectories. For continuous-time quasi-LPV systems, this requires differentiating through thousands of sequential fixed-step RK4 integration routines. This imposes extreme demands on computational graphs, memory buffers, and processor cache lines. Selecting the correct Automatic Differentiation (AD) framework is paramount to making the FIM initialization computationally tractable.PyTorch and Dynamic Computation GraphsPyTorch is built upon a dynamic, "Define-by-Run" computational graph. When utilizing reverse-mode automatic differentiation (backpropagation) in PyTorch, the framework must record the intermediate state of every single operation executed during the forward pass to compute the subsequent chain rule. Unrolling an RK4 integrator over thousands of timesteps generates a catastrophically deep computation graph. Storing four intermediate stages per timestep, across multiple states, batches, and shooting nodes, frequently leads to Out-Of-Memory (OOM) errors on modern GPUs. Furthermore, calculating a full Jacobian in reverse mode requires executing sequential Vector-Jacobian Products (VJPs) for every output dimension, which is exceptionally slow for long temporal trajectories.JAX and Just-In-Time CompilationJAX circumvents the dynamic graph memory explosion by utilizing Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation via Accelerated Linear Algebra (XLA). JAX statically analyzes and fuses the computational graph before execution, heavily optimizing memory allocation. Crucially, JAX provides highly optimized primitives for both reverse-mode (jax.jacrev) and forward-mode (jax.jacfwd) automatic differentiation.For a system with 13 physical parameters and a trajectory containing thousands of temporal output points, the parameter vector is significantly narrower than the output space. In this topology, forward-mode AD (executing Jacobian-Vector Products, or JVPs) is mathematically superior. Forward-mode AD propagates tangents forward alongside the RK4 integration, computing the sensitivity matrix simultaneously without ever needing to store the massive history of intermediate integration states required by reverse-mode AD. Utilizing jax.jacfwd allows for highly efficient, memory-safe computation of the full $s_c$ norms.CasADi and Operator OverloadingCasADi is a software framework engineered specifically for numerical optimal control, Nonlinear Programming (NLP), and Model Predictive Control (MPC). Unlike deep learning frameworks, CasADi does not rely on tape-based AD. Instead, it utilizes heavily optimized symbolic algorithmic differentiation based on operator overloading. CasADi builds sparse symbolic graphs (using SX or MX variable types) and topologically sorts the operations. It fundamentally understands the sparsity patterns inherent in multiple-shooting formulations and RK4 integrators.
