00020 [PDF p.1]                                                                                             Abstract


00021 [PDF p.1] 


00022 [PDF p.1]                                                        Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are a vital modeling technique that rely on


00023 [PDF p.1]                                                        internal states learned indirectly by optimization of a supervised, unsupervised, or


00024 [PDF p.1]                                                        reinforcement training loss. RNNs are used to model dynamic processes that are


00025 [PDF p.1]                                                        characterized by underlying latent states whose form is often unknown, precluding


00026 [PDF p.1]                                                        its analytic representation inside an RNN. In the Predictive-State Representation


00027 [PDF p.1]                                                        (PSR) literature, latent state processes are modeled by an internal state representa-


00028 [PDF p.1]                                                        tion that directly models the distribution of future observations, and most recent


00029 [PDF p.1]                                                        work in this area has relied on explicitly representing and targeting sufficient statis-


00030 [PDF p.1]                                                        tics of this probability distribution. We seek to combine the advantages of RNNs


00031 [PDF p.1]                                                        and PSRs by augmenting existing state-of-the-art recurrent neural networks with


00032 [PDF p.1]                                                        P REDICTIVE -S TATE D ECODERS (P SDs), which add supervision to the network’s


00033 [PDF p.1]                                                        internal state representation to target predicting future observations. P SDs are


00034 [PDF p.1]                                                        simple to implement and easily incorporated into existing training pipelines via


00035 [PDF p.1]                                                        additional loss regularization. We demonstrate the effectiveness of P SDs with


00036 [PDF p.1]                                                        experimental results in three different domains: probabilistic filtering, Imitation


00037 [PDF p.1]                                                        Learning, and Reinforcement Learning. In each, our method improves statistical


00038 [PDF p.1]                                                        performance of state-of-the-art recurrent baselines and does so with fewer iterations


00039 [PDF p.1]                                                        and less data.

00042 [PDF p.1]                                            1       Introduction


00043 [PDF p.1]                                            Despite their wide success in a variety of domains, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are often


00044 [PDF p.1]                                            inhibited by the difficulty of learning an internal state representation. Internal state is a unifying


00045 [PDF p.1]                                            characteristic of RNNs, as it serves as an RNN’s memory. Learning these internal states is challenging


00046 [PDF p.1]                                            because optimization is guided by the indirect signal of the RNN’s target task, such as maximizing


00047 [PDF p.1]                                            the cost-to-go for reinforcement learning or maximizing the likelihood of a sequence of words. These


00048 [PDF p.1]                                            target tasks have a latent state sequence that characterizes the underlying sequential data-generating


00049 [PDF p.1]                                            process. Unfortunately, most settings do not afford a parametric model of latent state that is available


00050 [PDF p.1]                                            to the learner.


00051 [PDF p.1]                                            However, recent work has shown that in certain settings, latent states can be characterized by


00052 [PDF p.1]                                            observations alone [8, 24, 26] – which are almost always available to recurrent models. In such


00053 [PDF p.1]                                            partially-observable problems (e.g. Fig. 1a), a single observation is not guaranteed to contain enough


00054 [PDF p.1]                                            information to fully represent the system’s latent state. For example, a single image of a robot is


00055 [PDF p.1]                                            insufficient to characterize its latent velocity and acceleration. While a latent state parametrization


00056 [PDF p.1]                                            may be known in some domains – e.g. a simple pendulum can be sufficiently modeled by its angle


00057 [PDF p.1]                                            and angular velocity (θ, θ̇) – data from most domains cannot be explicitly parametrized.

00071 [PDF p.2] In lieu of ground truth access to latent states, recurrent neural networks [32, 47] employ internal states


00072 [PDF p.2] to summarize previous data, serving as a learner’s memory. We avoid the terminology “hidden state"


00073 [PDF p.2] as it refers to the internal state in the RNN literature but refers to the latent state in the HMM, PSR,


00074 [PDF p.2] and related literature. Internal states are modified towards minimizing the target application’s loss,


00075 [PDF p.2] e.g., minimizing observation loss in filtering or cumulative reward in reinforcement learning. The


00076 [PDF p.2] target application’s loss is not directly defined over the internal states: they are updated via the chain


00077 [PDF p.2] rule (backpropagation) through the global loss. Although this modeling is indirect, recurrent networks


00078 [PDF p.2] nonetheless can achieve state-of-the-art results on many robotics [18, 23], vision [34, 50], and natural


00079 [PDF p.2] language tasks [15, 20, 38] when training succeeds. However, recurrent model optimization is


00080 [PDF p.2] hampered by two main difficulties: 1) non-convexity, and 2) the loss does not directly encourage


00081 [PDF p.2] the internal state to model the latent state. A poor internal state representation can yield poor task


00082 [PDF p.2] performance, but rarely does the task objective directly measure the quality of the internal state.


00083 [PDF p.2] Predictive-State Representations (PSRs) [8, 24, 26] offer an alternative internal state representation


00084 [PDF p.2] to that of RNNs in terms of the available observations. Spectral learning methods for PSRs provide


00085 [PDF p.2] theoretical guarantees on discovering the global optimum for the model and internal state parameters


00086 [PDF p.2] under the assumptions of infinite training data and realizability. However, in the non-realizable setting


00087 [PDF p.2] – i.e. model mismatch (e.g., using learned parameters of a linear system model for a non-linear system)


00088 [PDF p.2] – these algorithms lose any performance guarantees on using the learned model for the target inference


00089 [PDF p.2] tasks. Extensions to handle nonlinear systems rely on RKHS embeddings [43], which themselves


00090 [PDF p.2] can be computationally infeasible to use with large datasets. Nevertheless, when these models are


00091 [PDF p.2] trainable, they often achieve strong performance [24, 45]; the structure they impose significantly


00092 [PDF p.2] simplifies the learning problem.


00093 [PDF p.2] We leverage ideas from the both RNN and PSR paradigms, resulting in a marriage of two orthogonal


00094 [PDF p.2] sequential modeling approaches. When training an RNN, P REDICTIVE -S TATE D ECODERS (Fig. 1b)


00095 [PDF p.2] provide direct supervision on the internal state, aiding the training problem. The proposed method


00096 [PDF p.2] can be viewed as an instance of Multi-Task Learning (MTL) [13] and self-supervision [27], using the


00097 [PDF p.2] inputs to the learner to form a secondary unsupervised objective. Our contribution is a general method


00098 [PDF p.2] that improves performance of learning RNNs for sequential prediction problems. The approach is


00099 [PDF p.2] easy to implement as a regularizer on traditional RNN loss functions with little overhead and can


00100 [PDF p.2] thus be incorporated into a variety of existing recurrent models.


00101 [PDF p.2] In our experiments, we examine three domains where recurrent models are used to model temporal


00102 [PDF p.2] dependencies: probabilistic filtering, where we predict the future observation given past observations;


00103 [PDF p.2] Imitation Learning, where the learner attempts to mimic an expert’s actions; and Reinforcement


00104 [PDF p.2] Learning, where a policy is trained to maximize cumulative reward. We observe that our method


00105 [PDF p.2] improves loss convergence rates and results in higher-quality final objectives in these domains.

00140 [PDF p.3] 2.1        Recurrent Models and RNNs


00141 [PDF p.3] 


00142 [PDF p.3] A classical supervised machine learning approach for learning internal models involves choosing


00143 [PDF p.3] an explicit parametrization for the internal states and assuming ground-truth access to these states


00144 [PDF p.3] and observations at training time [17, 29, 33, 37]. These models focus on learning only the recursive


00145 [PDF p.3] model f in Fig. 2, assuming access to the st (Fig. 1a) at training time. Another class of approaches


00146 [PDF p.3] drop the assumption of access to ground truth but still assume a parametrization of the internal state.


00147 [PDF p.3] These models set up a multi-step prediction error and use expectation maximization to alternate


00148 [PDF p.3] between optimizing over the model’s parameters and the internal state values [2, 19, 16].


00149 [PDF p.3] While imposing a fixed representation on the internal state adds structure to the learning problem, it


00150 [PDF p.3] can limit performance. For many problems such as speech recognition [20] or text generation [48], it


00151 [PDF p.3] is difficult to fully represent a latent state inside the model’s internal state. Instead, typical machine


00152 [PDF p.3] learning solutions rely on the Recurrent Neural Network architecture. The RNN model (Fig. 2) uses


00153 [PDF p.3] the internal state to make predictions yt = f (ht , xt ) and is trained by minimizing a series of loss


00154 [PDF p.3] functions `t over each prediction, as shown in the following optimization problem:

00172 [PDF p.4] The general difficulty with the objective in Eq. (2) is that the recurrence with f results in a highly


00173 [PDF p.4] non-convex and difficult optimization [2].


00174 [PDF p.4] RNN models are thus often trained with backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) [55]. BPTT allows


00175 [PDF p.4] future losses incurred at timestep t to be back-propogated and affect the parameter updates to f .


00176 [PDF p.4] These updates to f then change the distribution of internal states computed during the next forward


00177 [PDF p.4] pass through time. The difficulty is then that small updates to f can drastically change the distribution


00178 [PDF p.4] of ht , sometimes resulting in error exponential in the time horizon [53]. This “diffusion problem"


00179 [PDF p.4] can yield an unstable training procedure with exponentially exploding or vanishing gradients [7].


00180 [PDF p.4] While techniques such as truncated gradients [47] or gradient-clipping [35] can alleviate some of


00181 [PDF p.4] these problems, each of these techniques yields stability by discarding information about how future


00182 [PDF p.4] observations and predictions should backpropagate through the current internal state. A significant


00183 [PDF p.4] innovation in training internal states with long-term dependence was the LSTM [25]. Many variants


00184 [PDF p.4] on LSTMs exist (e.g. GRUs [14]), yet in the domains evaluated by Greff et al. [21], none consistently


00185 [PDF p.4] exhibit statistically significant improvements over LSTMs.


00186 [PDF p.4] In the next section, we discuss a different paradigm for learning temporal models. In contrast with the


00187 [PDF p.4] open-ended internal-state learned by RNNs, Predictive-State methods do not parameterize a specific


00188 [PDF p.4] representation of the internal state but use certain assumptions to construct a mathematical structure


00189 [PDF p.4] in terms of the observations to find a globally optimal representation.

00191 [PDF p.4] 2.2   Predictive-State Models


00192 [PDF p.4] Predictive-State Representations (PSRs) address the problem of finding an internal state by for-


00193 [PDF p.4] mulating the representation directly in terms of observable quantities. Instead of targeting a pre-


00194 [PDF p.4] diction loss as with RNNs, PSRs define a belief over the distribution of k future observations,


00195 [PDF p.4] gt = [xTt , ..., xTt+k−1 ]T ∈ Rkn given all the past observations pt = [x0 , . . . xt−1 ] [10]. In the case of


00196 [PDF p.4] linear systems, this k is similar to the rank of the observability matrix [6]. The key assumption in


00197 [PDF p.4] PSRs is that the definition of state is equivalent to having sufficient information to predict everything


00198 [PDF p.4] about gt at time-step t [42], i.e. there is a bijective function that maps P (st |pt−1 ) – the distribution


00199 [PDF p.4] of latent state given the past – to P (gt |pt−1 ) – the belief over future observations.


00200 [PDF p.4] Spectral learning approaches were developed to find an globally optimal internal state representa-


00201 [PDF p.4] tion and the transition model f for these Predictive-State models. In the controls literature, these


00202 [PDF p.4] approaches were developed as subspace identification [51], and in the ML literature as spectral


00203 [PDF p.4] approaches for partially-observed systems [9, 8, 26, 56]. A significant improvement in model


00204 [PDF p.4] learning was developed by Boots et al. [10], Hefny et al. [24], where sufficient feature functions


00205 [PDF p.4] φ (e.g., moments) map distributions

00207 [PDF p.4] E [φ(gt )|pt ] = E gt , gt gtT |pt are the sufficient statistics for a Gaussian distribution. With this


00208 [PDF p.4] representation, learning latent state prediction models can be reduced to supervised learning.


00209 [PDF p.4] Hefny et al. [24] used this along with Instrumental Variable Regression [11] to develop a procedure

00257 [PDF p.5] is optimized by minimizing the weighted total loss function L + λR where λ is the weighting on

00259 [PDF p.5] sufficient for directly predicting sufficient future observations. Unlike more standard regularization

00262 [PDF p.5] Our method may be interpreted as an instance of Multi-Task Learning (MTL) [13]. MTL has found


00263 [PDF p.5] use in recent deep neural networks [5, 27, 30]. The idea of MTL is to employ a shared representation


00264 [PDF p.5] to perform complementary or similar tasks. When the learner exhibits good performance on one


00265 [PDF p.5] task, some of its understanding can be transferred to a related task. In our case, forcing RNNs to be


00266 [PDF p.5] able to more explicitly reason about the future they will encounter is an intuitive and general method.


00267 [PDF p.5] Endowing RNNs with a theoretically-motivated representation of the future better enables them


00268 [PDF p.5] to serve their purpose of making sequential predictions, resulting in more effective learning. This


00269 [PDF p.5] difference is pronounced in applications such as imitation and reinforcement learning (Sections 4.2


00270 [PDF p.5] and 4.3) where the primary objective is to find a control policy to maximize accumulated future


00271 [PDF p.5] reward while receiving only observations from the system. MTL with P SDs supervises the network


00272 [PDF p.5] to predict the future and implicitly the consequences of the learned policy. Finally, our P SD objective


00273 [PDF p.5] can be considered an instance of self-supervision [27] as it uses the inputs to the learner to form a


00274 [PDF p.5] secondary unsupervised objective.


00275 [PDF p.5] As discussed in Section 2.1, the purpose of the internal state in recurrent network models (RNNs,


00276 [PDF p.5] LSTMs, deep, or otherwise) is to capture a quantity similar to that of state. Ideally, the learner


00277 [PDF p.5] would be able to back-propagate through the primary objective function L and discover the best


00278 [PDF p.5] representation of the latent state of the system towards minimizing the objective. However, as this


00279 [PDF p.5] problem highly non-convex, BPTT often yields a locally-optimal solution in a basin determined by


00280 [PDF p.5] the initialization of the parameters and the dataset. By introducing R, the space of feasible models is


00281 [PDF p.5] reduced. We observe next how this objective leads our method to find better models.

00350 [PDF p.6] 4                     Experiments


00351 [PDF p.6] We present results on problems of increasing complexity for recurrent models: probabilistic filtering,


00352 [PDF p.6] Imitation Learning (IL), and Reinforcement Learning (RL). The first is easiest, as the goal is to predict


00353 [PDF p.6] the next future observation given the current observation and internal state. For imitation learning, the


00354 [PDF p.6] recurrent model is given training-time expert guidance with the goal of choosing actions to maximize


00355 [PDF p.6] the sequence of future rewards. Finally, we analyze the challenging domain of reinforcement learning,


00356 [PDF p.6] where the goal is the same as imitation learning but expert guidance is unavailable.


00357 [PDF p.6] P REDICTIVE -S TATE D ECODERS require two hyperparameters: k, the number of observations to


00358 [PDF p.6] characterize the predictive state and λ, the regularization trade-off factor. In most cases, we primarily


00359 [PDF p.6] tune λ, and set k to one of {2, . . . , 10}. For each domain, for each k, there were λ values for which


00360 [PDF p.6] the performance was worse than the baseline. However, for many sets of hyperparameters, the


00361 [PDF p.6] performance exceeded the baselines. Most notably, for many experiments, the convergence rate was


00362 [PDF p.6] significantly better using P SDs, implying that P SDs allows for more efficient data utilization for


00363 [PDF p.6] learning recurrent models.


00364 [PDF p.6] P SDs also require a specification of two other parameters in the architecture: the featurization function


00365 [PDF p.6] φ and decoding module F . For simplicity, we use an affine function as the decoder F in Eq. (4). The


00366 [PDF p.6] results presented below use an identity featurization φ for the presented results but include a short


00367 [PDF p.6] discussion of second order featurization. We find that in each domain, we are able to improve the


00368 [PDF p.6] performance of the state-of-the-art baselines. We observe improvements with both GRU and LSTM


00369 [PDF p.6] cells across a range of k and λ. In IL with P SDs, we come significantly closer and occasionally


00370 [PDF p.6] eclipse the expert’s performance, whereas the baselines never do. In our RL experiments, our method


00371 [PDF p.6] achieves statistically significant improvements over the state-of-the-art approach of [18, 41] on the 5


00372 [PDF p.6] different settings we tested.

00497 [PDF p.9] 5   Conclusion


00498 [PDF p.9] We introduced a theoretically-motivated method for improving the training of RNNs. Our method


00499 [PDF p.9] stems from previous literature that assigns statistical meaning to a learner’s internal state for modelling


00500 [PDF p.9] latent state of the data-generating processes. Our approach uses the objective in PSIMs and applies it to


00501 [PDF p.9] more complicated recurrent models such as LSTMs and GRUs and to objectives beyond probabilistic


00502 [PDF p.9] filtering such as imitation and reinforcement learning. We show that our straightforward method


00503 [PDF p.9] improves performance across all domains with which we experimented.
