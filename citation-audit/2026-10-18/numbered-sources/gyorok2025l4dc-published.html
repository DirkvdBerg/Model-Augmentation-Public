00025 [PDF p.1]                                                             Abstract


00026 [PDF p.1]       Deep-learning-based nonlinear system identification has shown the ability to produce reliable and


00027 [PDF p.1]       highly accurate models in practice. However, these black-box models lack physical interpretability,


00028 [PDF p.1]       and a considerable part of the learning effort is often spent on capturing already expected/known


00029 [PDF p.1]       behavior of the system, that can be accurately described by first-principles laws of physics. A po-


00030 [PDF p.1]       tential solution is to directly integrate such prior physical knowledge into the model structure, com-


00031 [PDF p.1]       bining the strengths of physics-based modeling and deep-learning-based identification. The most


00032 [PDF p.1]       common approach is to use an additive model augmentation structure, where the physics-based


00033 [PDF p.1]       and the machine-learning (ML) components are connected in parallel, i.e., additively. However,


00034 [PDF p.1]       such models are overparametrized, training them is challenging, potentially causing the physics-


00035 [PDF p.1]       based part to lose interpretability. To overcome this challenge, this paper proposes an orthogonal


00036 [PDF p.1]       projection-based regularization technique to enhance parameter learning and even model accuracy


00037 [PDF p.1]       in learning-based augmentation of nonlinear baseline models.


00038 [PDF p.1]       Keywords: Nonlinear system identification, Model augmentation, Hybrid modeling, Physics-


00039 [PDF p.1]       based learning

00041 [PDF p.1] 1. Introduction


00042 [PDF p.1] Continuously rising performance requirements and the growing complexity of systems resulted in


00043 [PDF p.1] high demand for nonlinear models that can accurately describe complex behavior. For example,


00044 [PDF p.1] reliable path planning and motion control of autonomous vehicles require accurate dynamic models.


00045 [PDF p.1] First-principle (FP) models for these systems can usually be obtained from physical knowledge and


00046 [PDF p.1] practical engineering insight. While FP modeling approaches offer models that can describe the


00047 [PDF p.1] main dynamic behavior of systems on a wide operating range, they often provide approximative


00048 [PDF p.1] descriptions of complex dynamical aspects such as aerodynamic forces, tire dynamics, or friction


00049 [PDF p.1] characteristics, which can become dominant under high-performance operation of the system.


00050 [PDF p.1]     As an alternative approach, data-driven nonlinear modeling methods (identification) have been


00051 [PDF p.1] developed, capable of providing highly accurate and consistent models of the system behavior. In


00052 [PDF p.1] particular, recent approaches utilizing state-space (SS) models based on deep artificial neural net-


00053 [PDF p.1] works (ANNs) have shown exceptional results (Masti and Bemporad, 2021). However, the use of


00054 [PDF p.1] such models in practical applications, such as trajectory planning and control raised some signifi-


00055 [PDF p.1] cant concerns, since they lack physical interpretation (Ljung, 2010). Furthermore, such ANN-based

00064 [PDF p.2] black-box models typically extrapolate inaccurately from training data and are more prone to over-


00065 [PDF p.2] fitting when trained on small datasets. Another drawback of black-box identification is that often


00066 [PDF p.2] a considerable time of the learning effort is spent on capturing already expected behavior due to


00067 [PDF p.2] first-principles-based understanding of some aspects of the system.


00068 [PDF p.2]      To address these challenges, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and physics-guided


00069 [PDF p.2] neural networks (PGNNs) were introduced in Raissi et al. (2019), and Daw et al. (2022), respec-


00070 [PDF p.2] tively. Both branches of model learning apply an additional term in the cost function, penaliz-


00071 [PDF p.2] ing when the ANN predictions deviate from priori-selected physical laws during model training


00072 [PDF p.2] (Karpatne et al., 2017). A more traditional approach for combining physics-based knowledge and


00073 [PDF p.2] data-driven identification techniques is (light) grey-box modeling. A wide range of methods has


00074 [PDF p.2] been developed and applied successfully in practice for years (Bohlin, 2006), mainly for linear


00075 [PDF p.2] time invariant (LTI) system representations. Light grey-box models utilize physics-based system


00076 [PDF p.2] descriptions and data-driven parameter estimation (Schoukens and Ljung, 2019).


00077 [PDF p.2]      Model augmentation, i.e., hybrid modeling, is a promising direction of utilizing physical knowl-


00078 [PDF p.2] edge in nonlinear system identification (Schön et al., 2022). By combining FP models with flexible


00079 [PDF p.2] learning components, (i) faster convergence and (ii) better accuracy can be achieved compared to


00080 [PDF p.2] black-box learning methods while producing (iii) physically interpretable models (Djeumou et al.,


00081 [PDF p.2] 2022). Furthermore, existing approaches can be incorporated into the model augmentation frame-


00082 [PDF p.2] work, e.g., physics-based loss functions can be utilized during model optimization (Daw et al.,


00083 [PDF p.2] 2022) and physical parameters can be co-estimated with the parameters of the learning component


00084 [PDF p.2] (Psichogios and Ungar, 1992).


00085 [PDF p.2]      This paper investigates learning for hybrid model augmentation when the model is composed of


00086 [PDF p.2] an FP model with an additive neural network part, and the objective is to jointly estimate the ANN


00087 [PDF p.2] parameters and the physical parameters based on measured data from the system. This formulation


00088 [PDF p.2] can produce models with properties (i-iii), however simultaneous tuning of the learning-based and


00089 [PDF p.2] physical parameters causes overparametrization and results in ”competing” submodels. Instead of


00090 [PDF p.2] only compensating for the unmodeled terms, the ML part can learn some of the known dynamics


00091 [PDF p.2] of the FP model as well. This can generate unrealistic parameters in the FP model, resulting in


00092 [PDF p.2] less interpretable models and even hurting extrapolation properties. Recent studies have addressed


00093 [PDF p.2] similar challenges in the context of PGNN-based feedforward control methods (Bolderman et al.,


00094 [PDF p.2] 2022, 2024), yet, this issue remains unsolved for nonlinear system identification. Orthogonaliza-


00095 [PDF p.2] tion is an attractive approach that we now generalize for nonlinear model learning, inspired by Kon


00096 [PDF p.2] et al. (2022). With this modification, the learning component is penalized for learning the known


00097 [PDF p.2] dynamics of the FP model, by forcing a particular form of orthogonality between the ML- and


00098 [PDF p.2] physics-based layers. The original approach is limited to FP models that are linear in their parame-


00099 [PDF p.2] ters; therefore, a generalized version of the method is also presented in this paper.


00100 [PDF p.2]      The main contributions of this work can be summarized as:


00101 [PDF p.2] C1 Generalization of an orthogonal projection-based regularization method (Kon et al., 2022) for


00102 [PDF p.2]       FP models that are nonlinear in the parameters, and integrating the approach to learning-based


00103 [PDF p.2]       model augmentation.


00104 [PDF p.2] C2 Efficient initialization and normalization schemes for the additive model augmentation.

00235 [PDF p.5] 3. Orthogonalization-based regularization


00236 [PDF p.5] Assuming that system (1) is part of the model set spanned by the parametrization of (3), there


00237 [PDF p.5] should exist a choice of parameter pairs (θ, η) for the additive model augmentation structure that


00238 [PDF p.5] realizes exactly the dynamical relations of the data-generating system. However, since fηANN and


00239 [PDF p.5] fθ are connected in parallel, i.e., additively, the optimal θ∗ , η∗ parameters that minimize (4) are not


00240 [PDF p.5] unique, i.e., different θ, η parameters can result in the same input-state behavior. This is generally


00241 [PDF p.5] called non-identifiability. For ease of understanding, Example 1 illustrates this non-uniqueness.


00242 [PDF p.5] Example 1 Consider an FP model with a state-transition function of fθ (x̂, u) = θx̂, and fηANN


00243 [PDF p.5] as an ANN. By the universal approximation properties, there exist choices of weights and biases


00244 [PDF p.5] such that fηANN (x̂, u) ≈ WL+1 x̂. Consequently, for a true state-transition map f (x, u) = θ∗ x, any


00245 [PDF p.5] (θ, WL+1 ) pair that satisfies WL+1 + θ = θ∗ is a global minimizer of (4).


00246 [PDF p.5]     A straightforward approach would be to define a Θ∗ ⊂ Rnθ +nη set that collects all parameter


00247 [PDF p.5] pairs that achieve an equivalent system representation with the data-generating system. Then, during


00248 [PDF p.5] model training, the aim would be to find any (θ∗ , η∗ ) ∈ Θ∗ . However, if the parameterization is non-


00249 [PDF p.5] identifiable, certain gradient directions become indistinguishable from one another, increasing the


00250 [PDF p.5] risk of convergence to local minima. While stochastic gradient descent-based optimizers inherently


00251 [PDF p.5] provide some degree of implicit regularization that helps to mitigate this issue (Zhang et al., 2017),


00252 [PDF p.5] another critical concern arises: the physical parameters may converge to unrealistic values due to


00253 [PDF p.5] the non-uniqueness of θ∗ . As a result, the estimated FP model cannot reliably serve as a standalone


00254 [PDF p.5] physical model of the system. Additionally, physics-based models are often capable of extrapolating


00255 [PDF p.5] accurately beyond the range of observed data, while ANNs extrapolate poorly outside of the training


00256 [PDF p.5] data set. Hence, the augmented model can lose this advantageous extrapolation capability due to


00257 [PDF p.5] the indistinguishability of the model contributions in the cost during training.

00488 [PDF p.9] 5.2. Data acquisition


00489 [PDF p.9] To generate data for training, a high-fidelity multi-body simulator, the MuJoCo physics engine is


00490 [PDF p.9] utilized (Todorov et al., 2012). In this simulator, the digital twin model of the car was assembled


00491 [PDF p.9] using parameters that were identified based on measurements with the real car. Figure 2 shows the


00492 [PDF p.9] digital twin model. Multiple experiments were performed in the simulator to acquire sufficient data.


00493 [PDF p.9] The MuJoCo model operates with motion states in terms of joint positions and velocities, utilizing


00494 [PDF p.9] complex contact models and friction characteristics. The logged signals have been determined based


00495 [PDF p.9] on the states of the FP model. All states are directly measured, as it would be for the real F1Tenth.


00496 [PDF p.9] Two different trajectories (a lemniscate and a circular path), with 12 different velocity references


00497 [PDF p.9] have been utilized to achieve robust identification results. With a sampling frequency of fs = 40 Hz,


00498 [PDF p.9] altogether N = 15985 data points have been recorded. Half of the measurements were separated


00499 [PDF p.9] for model training, while the other half were used for testing. Trajectories with alternating reference


00500 [PDF p.9] velocities have been included in both. Lastly, 20% of each training trajectory have been randomly


00501 [PDF p.9] selected to form a validation data set, achieving an 80%-20% ratio of the training and validation


00502 [PDF p.9] data points. The gathered data does not contain any noise, but to test the methodology with noise


00503 [PDF p.9] levels that are typical for this application type, i.i.d. white Gaussian noise is added to the output


00504 [PDF p.9] signals in the training and validation data sets to reach Signal-to-noise ratio3 (SNR) values of 30 dB


00505 [PDF p.9] and 25 dB. To ensure a clear comparison of the resulting models, the test data is kept noise-free.

00509 [PDF p.9] ing to roughly 1.5 times the largest characteristic time scale of the data-generating system. The


00510 [PDF p.9] hyperparameters of fηANN have been determined empirically: 2 hidden layers with 64 nodes per


00511 [PDF p.9] layer have been applied, using the hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activation function. The regularization


00512 [PDF p.9] coefficient β was also tuned empirically, as illustrated in Figure 3, with β = 10−7 selected for the


00513 [PDF p.9] noiseless case. For scenarios with output noise, β waschosen using a similar approach. Based on

00547 [PDF p.10] 5.4. Results


00548 [PDF p.10] Model performance is evaluated using the Normalized Root Mean Square (NRMS) error, see, e.g.,


00549 [PDF p.10] Beintema et al. (2021), with results summarized in Table 1. As shown, augmenting the baseline


00550 [PDF p.10] model while keeping the physical parameters fixed leads to a significant improvement in accuracy


00551 [PDF p.10] compared to the standalone FP model. Jointly optimizing the physical and ANN parameters has


00552 [PDF p.10] further reduced the test error, while using the orthogonalization-based cost function led to additional


00553 [PDF p.10] improvements in model accuracy across all noise conditions. With increasing noise levels, the


00554 [PDF p.10] effect of orthogonal regularization on model accuracy is less noticeable compared to the noiseless


00555 [PDF p.10] scenario, but this is expected, as it is harder to separate the baseline model dynamics and the noise.


00556 [PDF p.10]      When the physical and learning-based parameters are co-estimated, we acquire a (θ̂, η̂) param-


00557 [PDF p.10] eter pair as a result of the optimization. This allows for evaluating the FP model separately using the


00558 [PDF p.10] estimated physical parameters θ̂, providing insight into the interpretability of the augmented model.


00559 [PDF p.10] Table 1 also includes these test results with the FP model, using θ̂. Without regularization, the


00560 [PDF p.10] physics-based component of the augmentation structure shows a notable drop in accuracy compared


00561 [PDF p.10] to the nominal baseline model, supporting our earlier statement that, in the absence of the proposed


00562 [PDF p.10] regularization, physical parameters may be tuned to unrealistic values, potentially undermining the


00563 [PDF p.10] interpretability of the augmented model. In contrast, applying orthogonal regularization not only


00564 [PDF p.10] enhances the overall accuracy of the augmented model but also improves the performance of the


00565 [PDF p.10] physics-based part relative to the nominal FP model. This demonstrates that the proposed method


00566 [PDF p.10] effectively promotes the desired complementarity between the baseline and learning components.


00567 [PDF p.10]      Comparing the results to a state-of-the-art black-box method, it is visible that even with the


00568 [PDF p.10] orthogonal projection-based approach, black-box models have performed slightly better. However,


00569 [PDF p.10] as we have demonstrated, model augmentation generates physically interpretable models in contrast


00570 [PDF p.10] to black-box approaches. Consequently, a slight reduction in model accuracy is an acceptable trade-


00571 [PDF p.10] off for the added interpretability.


00572 [PDF p.10] 6. Conclusion


00573 [PDF p.10] This paper introduced an orthogonal projection-based regularization method that extends the ap-


00574 [PDF p.10] proach in Kon et al. (2022), originally limited to linear-in-the-parameter FP models, to support


00575 [PDF p.10] models that are nonlinear in the parameters and improve the efficiency of estimating additive model


00576 [PDF p.10] augmentation structures. The proposed method improved model performance across various out-


00577 [PDF p.10] put noise levels. Although the impact of online recalculation of the projection matrix has not been


00578 [PDF p.10] evaluated, its potential benefits, despite the increased computational cost, will be explored in future


00579 [PDF p.10] studies. Further theoretical analysis and application in other model augmentation settings will be


00580 [PDF p.10] addressed in future research.
