00061 [PDF p.3]

00063 [PDF p.3] 


00064 [PDF p.3] Industrial motion control of dual–drive gantry stages is usually performed by position controllers acting


00065 [PDF p.3] 


00066 [PDF p.3] independently on each actuator. This approach neglects the unbalance and the mechanical coupling between


00067 [PDF p.3] 


00068 [PDF p.3] actuators, leading to poor positioning performances. To overcome this drawback, a model–based decoupling


00069 [PDF p.3] 


00070 [PDF p.3] control is proposed. Initially, a dynamic model of the gantry stage is proposed. Once identified and validated,


00071 [PDF p.3] 


00072 [PDF p.3] such model is written in terms of a decoupling basis. Then, by model inversion, a feedbackfeedforward


00073 [PDF p.3] 


00074 [PDF p.3] decoupling control is presented. Experimental results show that, in comparison to the independent axis control


00075 [PDF p.3] 


00076 [PDF p.3] approach, the proposed solution leads to improved motion control.

00087 [PDF p.4] 1    INTRODUCTION


00088 [PDF p.4] 


00089 [PDF p.4] A dual-drive gantry stage consists of a cross–arm, which is mounted on two linear actuators (X1 – and X2 – axes)


00090 [PDF p.4] 


00091 [PDF p.4] installed in parallel. The cross–arm serves as a support for a third linear actuator (Y – axis) carrying the payload.


00092 [PDF p.4] 


00093 [PDF p.4] This payload can be an additional actuator (Z – axis), as in Fig. 1; a camera; or any other device. The


00094 [PDF p.4] 


00095 [PDF p.4] connections between the cross-arm and the moving parts of actuators X1 and X2 are provided by flexible joints in


00096 [PDF p.4] 


00097 [PDF p.4] order to avoid high stresses and strains at the fixation interfaces. Thanks to their particular mechanical


00098 [PDF p.4] 


00099 [PDF p.4] configuration and to the advances in electrical linear drives, gantry stages have a very high precision over


00100 [PDF p.4] 


00101 [PDF p.4] workspace ratio and high dynamic performances. For this reason, they are used on high-end industrial


00102 [PDF p.4] 


00103 [PDF p.4] applications such as surface chip mounting, precision metrology, flat panel display manufacturing and


00104 [PDF p.4] 


00105 [PDF p.4] inspection, and many others.


00106 [PDF p.4] 


00107 [PDF p.4] 


00108 [PDF p.4] 


00109 [PDF p.4] Despite the advantages of advanced linear actuation devices, gantry stages cannot be used at its maximum level


00110 [PDF p.4] 


00111 [PDF p.4] of performance due to the coupling between the actuators constituting them. This coupling comprises the non-


00112 [PDF p.4] 


00113 [PDF p.4] uniform load distribution between actuators X1 and X2 due to the variable position of the payload along the cross-


00114 [PDF p.4] 


00115 [PDF p.4] arm; the coupling between actuators X1 and X2 due to different drive and motor characteristics between them;


00116 [PDF p.4] 


00117 [PDF p.4] and the acceleration of actuator Y. The latter generates a twisting moment about the center of the cross-arm, and


00118 [PDF p.4] 


00119 [PDF p.4] thus a force disturbance on actuators X1 and X2. All these factors induce desynchronization between the axes


00120 [PDF p.4] 


00121 [PDF p.4] carrying the cross-arm and, by extension, tool-point positioning errors.


00122 [PDF p.4] 


00123 [PDF p.4] 


00124 [PDF p.4] 


00125 [PDF p.4] In the literature, many efforts have been made towards a more advanced control of gantry stages. In general, the


00126 [PDF p.4] 


00127 [PDF p.4] actuators carrying the cross-arm are modeled as two separate entities and the coupling between them is


00128 [PDF p.4] 


00129 [PDF p.4] considered as an external disturbance affecting each. The objective of the proposed controllers is to reject such a


00130 [PDF p.4] 


00131 [PDF p.4] disturbance and to improve the synchronization between the actuators carrying the cross-arm. Essentially, three


00132 [PDF p.4] 


00133 [PDF p.4] types of control schemes can be found (Giam, Tan, & Huang, 2007): master/slave, master/master, and cross-


00134 [PDF p.4] 


00135 [PDF p.4] coupled. In the master/slave scheme, the actuators carrying the cross-arm are controlled independently and the


00136 [PDF p.4] 


00137 [PDF p.4] output of the master control-loop is used as the reference of the slave one. This type of scheme is used on gantry


00138 [PDF p.4] 


00139 [PDF p.4] stages where the path-tracking precision is a secondary objective, e.g. a gantry crane, as the slave motor will be


00140 [PDF p.4] 


00141 [PDF p.4] always delayed and therefore desynchronized from the master motor. In the master/master control scheme, the


00142 [PDF p.4] 


00143 [PDF p.4] actuators carrying the cross-arm are controlled independently and share the same set-point reference. This


00144 [PDF p.4] 


00145 [PDF p.4] control scheme is used in most industrial gantry stages. To compensate for the differences in the dynamics of the


00146 [PDF p.4] 


00147 [PDF p.5] motors or in their load, each independent control-loop is tuned heuristically to provide the best possible


00148 [PDF p.5] 


00149 [PDF p.5] positioning performances. Based on a dynamic model of a cross-arm carrying a moving payload, (Teo, Tan, Lim,


00150 [PDF p.5] 


00151 [PDF p.5] Huang, & Tay, 2007) proposes an adaptative tuning method for the PID controllers of the master/master scheme.


00152 [PDF p.5] 


00153 [PDF p.5] This control algorithm is aimed to compensate for the non-uniform load distribution between the actuators


00154 [PDF p.5] 


00155 [PDF p.5] carrying the cross-arm due to the moving payload. Finally, the cross-coupled control scheme is the one that has


00156 [PDF p.5] 


00157 [PDF p.5] received the most attention in the literature. In this scheme, the motors carrying the cross-arm are controlled


00158 [PDF p.5] 


00159 [PDF p.5] using the master/master control scheme. A third controller, placed between the independent control-loops, is


00160 [PDF p.5] 


00161 [PDF p.5] used to monitor and to compensate the desynchronization between the motors carrying the cross-arm by


00162 [PDF p.5] 


00163 [PDF p.5] accelerating the slowest one and by slowing down the fastest one. The structure and/or the tuning method of this


00164 [PDF p.5] 


00165 [PDF p.5] third controller can be of type optimal control (Tan, Lim, Huang, Dou, & Giam, 2004) (Hu, Yao, & Wang,


00166 [PDF p.5] 


00167 [PDF p.5] 2010), iterative learning control (Van Dijk, Tinsel, & Schrijver, 2001), fuzzy logic control (Lin & Shen, 2006),


00168 [PDF p.5] 


00169 [PDF p.5] or sliding mode control (Giam, Tan, & Huang, 2007)(Lin, Chou, Chen, & Lin, 2011).


00170 [PDF p.5] 


00171 [PDF p.5] 


00172 [PDF p.5] 


00173 [PDF p.5] The cross-coupled control scheme is essentially a non-parametric scheme, or a partially parametric one, based on


00174 [PDF p.5] 


00175 [PDF p.5] a simplified representation of the actuators dynamics without explicit modeling of the coupling between them.


00176 [PDF p.5] 


00177 [PDF p.5] Due to its nature, this approach has the drawback of being structurally complex and hard to compute, which


00178 [PDF p.5] 


00179 [PDF p.5] makes it difficult to implement in real-time applications. As a result, it is seldom used to control gantry stages


00180 [PDF p.5] 


00181 [PDF p.5] industrially. In the current paper, it is shown that the coupling effect, when adequately addressed, can be


00182 [PDF p.5] 


00183 [PDF p.5] included into the definition of a model-based decoupling control scheme that is structurally simple and therefore


00184 [PDF p.5] 


00185 [PDF p.5] well adapted to real-time implementation.


00186 [PDF p.5] 


00187 [PDF p.5] 


00188 [PDF p.5] 


00189 [PDF p.5] The first step to develop such a control scheme consists in building a detailed dynamic model of the gantry stage.


00190 [PDF p.5] 


00191 [PDF p.5] This model, which is presented in section 2, includes the payload (Y – axis), the cross-arm, and the actuators


00192 [PDF p.5] 


00193 [PDF p.5] carrying it (X1 – and X2 – axes). This detailed modeling accounts for the coupling effects associated with the


00194 [PDF p.5] 


00195 [PDF p.5] interaction between the actuators of the gantry stage, which has not been implemented before on a single model


00196 [PDF p.5] 


00197 [PDF p.5] to the best of the authors’ knowledge.


00198 [PDF p.5] 


00199 [PDF p.5] 


00200 [PDF p.5] 


00201 [PDF p.5] Concerning the control, the core of the proposed approach consists in expressing the proposed model, which is


00202 [PDF p.5] 


00203 [PDF p.5] coupled, in terms of the decoupled motions defined by the translation of the payload, the translation of, and the


00204 [PDF p.5] 


00205 [PDF p.5] rotation about, the center of mass of the gantry stage; and to use independent controllers to control each. The


00206 [PDF p.5] 


00207 [PDF p.5] method to obtain such a transformation between model representations is not usual. Indeed, if one wants to


00208 [PDF p.5] 


00209 [PDF p.6] transform the coupled model of the gantry stage into a decoupled one by interpreting it as an eigenvalue


00210 [PDF p.6] 


00211 [PDF p.6] problem, he will find that the gantry stage has two repeated eigenvalues (associated with the rigid motion of the


00212 [PDF p.6] 


00213 [PDF p.6] payload and the synchronizing motion of actuators X1 and X2). As a result, the orthogonalization has to be forced


00214 [PDF p.6] 


00215 [PDF p.6] by using an iterative method such as the Gram-Schmidt process, the Householder transformation, or the Givens


00216 [PDF p.6] 


00217 [PDF p.6] rotation. These methods, although effective, are known to be expensive to compute, and in some cases, to have


00218 [PDF p.6] 


00219 [PDF p.6] numerical stability issues (Higham, 2002). To avoid such drawbacks and to obtain a simpler transition between


00220 [PDF p.6] 


00221 [PDF p.6] model representations, the decoupling transformation proposed in this paper has two stages.


00222 [PDF p.6] 


00223 [PDF p.6] 


00224 [PDF p.6] 


00225 [PDF p.6] The first stage consists in rejecting the disturbance induced by the acceleration of the payload on actuators X1


00226 [PDF p.6] 


00227 [PDF p.6] and X2 by feedforward compensation. This is equivalent to eliminate one of the repeated eigenvalues from the


00228 [PDF p.6] 


00229 [PDF p.6] coupled model. Then, by using a modal transformation, the remaining coupled dynamics is defined as the


00230 [PDF p.6] 


00231 [PDF p.6] dynamics of a unique rigid body translating and pivoting with respect to its own center of mass; two motions that


00232 [PDF p.6] 


00233 [PDF p.6] are naturally decoupled. Based on the latter, a decoupling feedforward/feedback control scheme is derived by


00234 [PDF p.6] 


00235 [PDF p.6] model inversion.


00236 [PDF p.6] 


00237 [PDF p.6]

00240 [PDF p.6] 


00241 [PDF p.6]

00244 [PDF p.6] 


00245 [PDF p.6] Then, the development of the dynamic model of the gantry stage, along with an adapted parameter identification


00246 [PDF p.6] 


00247 [PDF p.6] method, is proposed. The simplification that allows decoupling the dynamics of the payload from the rest of the


00248 [PDF p.6] 


00249 [PDF p.6] system is introduced in this section as it is also exploited for parameter identification. Finally, a comparison


00250 [PDF p.6] 


00251 [PDF p.6] between simulation and experiment is presented and the influence of un-modeled phenomena on the overall


00252 [PDF p.6] 


00253 [PDF p.6] system behavior is discussed.


00254 [PDF p.6]

00276 [PDF p.7] 2.1    Mechanical description of gantry stages


00277 [PDF p.7] 


00278 [PDF p.7] 


00279 [PDF p.7] A gantry stage is a 3–DOF high–speed, high–precision Cartesian positioning system. It consists of a cross-arm


00280 [PDF p.7] 


00281 [PDF p.7] that is moved by two parallel linear actuators (axes X1 and X2). To provide high–power density actuation, axes X1


00282 [PDF p.7] 


00283 [PDF p.7] and X2 are often equipped with iron–core Permanent Magnet Linear Synchronous Machines (PMLSM). The


00284 [PDF p.7] 


00285 [PDF p.7] moving cross-arm serves as a support for a third iron–core PMLSM (Y–axis). The tool–point or Z–axis is


00286 [PDF p.7] 


00287 [PDF p.7] mounted orthogonally to the Y–axis. The tool–point is usually installed by the customer according to its needs.


00288 [PDF p.7] 


00289 [PDF p.7] To keep this study general, the mass of the Y-axis moving carriage and those of the tool-point are considered


00290 [PDF p.7] 


00291 [PDF p.7] one. Each actuator is equipped with a linear encoder to measure its position. The travel length of the actuators


00292 [PDF p.7] 


00293 [PDF p.7] can vary from a few centimeters to several meters depending on the application and required work area. To limit


00294 [PDF p.7] 


00295 [PDF p.7] stresses at the cross-arm to actuator junctions and to permit a small desynchronization between the parallel


00296 [PDF p.7] 


00297 [PDF p.7] actuators X1 and X2, flexible joints are used.


00298 [PDF p.7] 


00299 [PDF p.7] 


00300 [PDF p.7] 


00301 [PDF p.7] Concerning the installation, gantry stages can be mounted on different types of bases. This is done in function of


00302 [PDF p.7] 


00303 [PDF p.7] the required level of precision. For an increasing level of precision, the typical base types that are used are;


00304 [PDF p.7] 


00305 [PDF p.7] welded, die-cast, and granite for very high-precision applications.

00920 [PDF p.16] 3.1       Transformation into a decoupling basis


00921 [PDF p.16] Many authors have addressed the motion control of gantry stages from the perspective of independent axis


00922 [PDF p.16] 


00923 [PDF p.16] control. That is, the actuators carrying the cross-arm are controlled as two separate entities and the coupling


00924 [PDF p.16] 


00925 [PDF p.16] effect is considered as an external disturbance affecting each. Cross-coupled controllers are added to minimize


00926 [PDF p.16] 


00927 [PDF p.16] both the settling time of the tool–point along the X-direction and the synchronization error ( X1 – X2 ). However,


00928 [PDF p.16] 


00929 [PDF p.16] the principal drawback of control schemes based on an independent axis control approach is not the coupling


00930 [PDF p.16] 


00931 [PDF p.16] effect itself, but the fact that the actuators carrying the cross-arm are confronted with two antagonistic objectives.


00932 [PDF p.16] 


00933 [PDF p.16] Indeed, they have to displace the cross-arm in minimum time; and they have to do so while moving in


00934 [PDF p.16] 


00935 [PDF p.16] synchronization (i.e. by minimizing the cross-arm’s yaw rotation). Physically, the translation and yaw rotation of


00936 [PDF p.16] 


00937 [PDF p.16] the cross-arm have very different dynamics behavior. For this reason, it is very difficult to tune the independent


00938 [PDF p.16] 


00939 [PDF p.16] pairs “controller-actuator” to satisfy both objectives. In industry, each independent control-loop is tuned


00940 [PDF p.16] 


00941 [PDF p.16] heuristically to provide the best possible positioning performances.


00942 [PDF p.16] 


00943 [PDF p.16] 


00944 [PDF p.16] 


00945 [PDF p.16] A solution to bypass this inherent drawback of independent axis control and to harness the full potential of


00946 [PDF p.16] 


00947 [PDF p.16] gantry stages is to use Independent Modal Space Control (IMSC) (Meirovitch, 1990) (Bagordo, Cazzulani,

00949 [PDF p.16] Resta, & Ripamonti, 2011). The principle of this approach consists in transforming the mechanically coupled


00950 [PDF p.16]

01321 [PDF p.22] The first test consists in synchronously displacing X1 and X2 from –0.2 m to +0.2 m at a maximum velocity of


01322 [PDF p.22] 


01323 [PDF p.22] 2 m/s and acceleration of 20 m/s². For the first motion example (Fig.5), position references are generated with a


01324 [PDF p.22] 


01325 [PDF p.22] 26.5 ms jerk time, the payload is kept immobile at 0.2 m. In this configuration of the Y–axis, the unbalance


01326 [PDF p.22] 


01327 [PDF p.22] between X1 and X2 actuators is at its maximum value.


01328 [PDF p.22] 


01329 [PDF p.22] 


01330 [PDF p.22] 


01331 [PDF p.22] The second test consists in displacing the payload along a diagonal over the machine workspace. Trajectories for


01332 [PDF p.22] 


01333 [PDF p.22] axes X1, X2, and Y are identical. They move from –0.2 m to +0.2 m at a maximum velocity of 2 m/s and a


01334 [PDF p.22] 


01335 [PDF p.22] maximum acceleration of 20 m/s². During this motion, the gantry stage goes from an extreme unbalance


01336 [PDF p.22] 


01337 [PDF p.22] condition to the other. Due to the acceleration of the payload, the coupling between the Y–axis and actuators X1


01338 [PDF p.22] 


01339 [PDF p.22] and X2 is important. To exacerbate this phenomenon, the jerk time has been set to zero for all axes (Fig.6).


01340 [PDF p.22] 


01341 [PDF p.22] 


01342 [PDF p.22] 


01343 [PDF p.22] Figs. 5 and 6, allow verifying that controlling the gantry stage on a decoupling basis results in an improvement


01344 [PDF p.22] 


01345 [PDF p.22] of the synchronization of actuators X1 and X2 and in a reduction of more than 50% of the position error during


01346 [PDF p.22] 


01347 [PDF p.22] acceleration shifts. Moreover, when jerk time is well tuned (Fig.5), the settling time is significantly reduced, i.e.


01348 [PDF p.22] 


01349 [PDF p.22] a reduction of about 10%, compared to the independent axis control. The remaining oscillations on the


01350 [PDF p.22] 


01351 [PDF p.22] synchronization and on position tracking errors are mostly due to the detent forces of the PMLSMs.


01352 [PDF p.22] 


01353 [PDF p.22]

01356 [PDF p.22]

01695 [PDF p.26] 


01696 [PDF p.26] In this paper, a model-based decoupling control method for dual-drive gantry stages is presented. First, a


01697 [PDF p.26] 


01698 [PDF p.26] dynamical model of the gantry stage is presented in terms of coordinates (X, Θ, Y). Where, X and Θ define,


01699 [PDF p.26] 


01700 [PDF p.26] respectively, the translation and the rotation of the transverse member of the gantry stage, Y defines the


01701 [PDF p.26] 


01702 [PDF p.26] translation of the payload. This model accounts for the coupling effects associated with the interaction between


01703 [PDF p.26] 


01704 [PDF p.26] the actuators constituting the gantry stage. To make this model adaptable to various types of gantry stages, an


01705 [PDF p.26] 


01706 [PDF p.26] identification method (that can be easily implemented using independent axis control) is also presented. The


01707 [PDF p.26] 


01708 [PDF p.26] second part of the paper is devoted to the model-based decoupling control of gantry stages. As described, via


01709 [PDF p.26] 


01710 [PDF p.26] feedforward compensation, it is possible to fully decouple the dynamics of the payload from the rest of the


01711 [PDF p.26] 


01712 [PDF p.27] system. Thanks to this pre-decoupling, the remaining system dynamics (defined now in terms of coordinates X


01713 [PDF p.27] 


01714 [PDF p.27] and Θ) can be easily decoupled by interpreting it as an eigenvalue problem. Without this pre-decoupling, the


01715 [PDF p.27] 


01716 [PDF p.27] orthogonalization of the motion equations of the gantry stage would have been more laborious and


01717 [PDF p.27] 


01718 [PDF p.27] computationally more expensive due to the repeated eigenvalues associated with the rigid motions X and Y. The


01719 [PDF p.27] 


01720 [PDF p.27] decoupled model that is obtained using the pre-decoupling approach is also a physical model. It describes the


01721 [PDF p.27] 


01722 [PDF p.27] dynamics of the gantry stage in terms of the total mass in translation and in rotation; two motions that are


01723 [PDF p.27] 


01724 [PDF p.27] naturally decoupled. Based on this description, a decoupling feedforward/feedback control scheme was derived


01725 [PDF p.27] 


01726 [PDF p.27] by model inversion. Experimental results show that in comparison to the industrial independent axis control, the


01727 [PDF p.27] 


01728 [PDF p.27] proposed model-based decoupling control lead to a significant improvement of the positioning performance of


01729 [PDF p.27] 


01730 [PDF p.27] the gantry stage.


01731 [PDF p.27] 


01732 [PDF p.27] 


01733 [PDF p.27] 


01734 [PDF p.27] The contribution of this paper is by no means limited to the model-based decoupling control method that is


01735 [PDF p.27] 


01736 [PDF p.27] presented here. Indeed, the proposed modeling of the gantry stage, along with the proposed identification


01737 [PDF p.27] 


01738 [PDF p.27] method, can be used as a base to develop other control architectures. This will be the subject of future papers.
