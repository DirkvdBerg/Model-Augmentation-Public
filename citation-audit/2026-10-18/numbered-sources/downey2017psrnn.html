00028 [PDF p.1]                                                                                           Abstract


00029 [PDF p.1] 


00030 [PDF p.1]                                                     We present a new model, Predictive State Recurrent Neural Networks (PSRNNs),


00031 [PDF p.1]                                                     for filtering and prediction in dynamical systems. PSRNNs draw on insights from


00032 [PDF p.1]                                                     both Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Predictive State Representations


00033 [PDF p.1]                                                     (PSRs), and inherit advantages from both types of models. Like many successful


00034 [PDF p.1]                                                     RNN architectures, PSRNNs use (potentially deeply composed) bilinear transfer


00035 [PDF p.1]                                                     functions to combine information from multiple sources. We show that such bilinear


00036 [PDF p.1]                                                     functions arise naturally from state updates in Bayes filters like PSRs, in which


00037 [PDF p.1]                                                     observations can be viewed as gating belief states. We also show that PSRNNs


00038 [PDF p.1]                                                     can be learned effectively by combining Backpropogation Through Time (BPTT)


00039 [PDF p.1]                                                     with an initialization derived from a statistically consistent learning algorithm


00040 [PDF p.1]                                                     for PSRs called two-stage regression (2SR). Finally, we show that PSRNNs can


00041 [PDF p.1]                                                     be factorized using tensor decomposition, reducing model size and suggesting


00042 [PDF p.1]                                                     interesting connections to existing multiplicative architectures such as LSTMs. We


00043 [PDF p.1]                                                     applied PSRNNs to 4 datasets, and showed that we outperform several popular


00044 [PDF p.1]                                                     alternative approaches to modeling dynamical systems in all cases.

00047 [PDF p.1]                                            1   Introduction


00048 [PDF p.1] 


00049 [PDF p.1]                                            Learning to predict temporal sequences of observations is a fundamental challenge in a range of


00050 [PDF p.1]                                            disciplines including machine learning, robotics, and natural language processing. While there are a


00051 [PDF p.1]                                            wide variety of different approaches to modelling time series data, many of these approaches can be


00052 [PDF p.1]                                            categorized as either recursive Bayes Filtering or Recurrent Neural Networks.


00053 [PDF p.1]                                            Bayes Filters (BFs) [1] focus on modeling and maintaining a belief state: a set of statistics, which,


00054 [PDF p.1]                                            if known at time t, are sufficient to predict all future observations as accurately as if we know the


00055 [PDF p.1]                                            full history. The belief state is generally interpreted as the statistics of a distribution over the latent


00056 [PDF p.1]                                            state of the data generating process conditioned on history. BFs recursively update the belief state by


00057 [PDF p.1] 


00058 [PDF p.2] conditioning on new observations using Bayes rule. Examples of common BFs include sequential


00059 [PDF p.2] filtering in Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) [2] and Kalman Filters (KFs) [3].


00060 [PDF p.2] Predictive State Representations [4] (PSRs) are a variation on Bayes filters that do not define system


00061 [PDF p.2] state explicitly, but proceed directly to a representation of state as the statistics of a distribution


00062 [PDF p.2] of features of future observations, conditioned on history. By defining the belief state in terms of


00063 [PDF p.2] observables rather than latent states, PSRs can be easier to learn than other filtering methods [5–7].


00064 [PDF p.2] PSRs also support rich functional forms through kernel mean map embeddings [8], and a natural


00065 [PDF p.2] interpretation of model update behavior as a gating mechanism. We note this last is not unique to


00066 [PDF p.2] PSRs, as it is also possible to interpret the model updates of other BFs such as HMMs in terms of


00067 [PDF p.2] gating.


00068 [PDF p.2] Due to their probabilistic grounding, BFs and PSRs possess a strong statistical theory leading


00069 [PDF p.2] to efficient learning algorithms. In particular, method-of-moments algorithms provide consistent


00070 [PDF p.2] parameter estimates for a range of BFs including PSRs [5, 7, 9–11]. Unfortunately, current versions


00071 [PDF p.2] of method of moments initialization restrict BFs to relatively simple functional forms such as linear-


00072 [PDF p.2] Gaussian (KFs) or linear-multinomial (HMMs).


00073 [PDF p.2] Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are an alternative to BFs that model sequential data via a


00074 [PDF p.2] parameterized internal state and update function. In contrast to BFs, RNNs are directly trained to


00075 [PDF p.2] minimize output prediction error, without adhering to any axiomatic probabilistic interpretation.


00076 [PDF p.2] Examples of popular RNN models include Long-Short Term Memory networks [12] (LSTMs), Gated


00077 [PDF p.2] Recurrent Units [13] (GRUs), and simple recurrent networks such as Elman networks [14].


00078 [PDF p.2] RNNs have several advantages over BFs. Their flexible functional form supports large, rich models.


00079 [PDF p.2] And, RNNs can be paired with simple gradient-based training procedures that achieve state-of-the-art


00080 [PDF p.2] performance on many tasks [15]. RNNs have drawbacks however: unlike BFs, RNNs lack an


00081 [PDF p.2] axiomatic probabilistic interpretation, and are therefore difficult to analyze. Furthermore, despite


00082 [PDF p.2] strong performance in some domains, RNNs are notoriously difficult to train; in particular it is


00083 [PDF p.2] difficult to find good initializations.


00084 [PDF p.2] In summary, RNNs and BFs offer complementary advantages and disadvantages: RNNs offer rich


00085 [PDF p.2] functional forms at the cost of statistical insight, while BFs possess a sophisticated statistical theory


00086 [PDF p.2] but are restricted to simpler functional forms in order to maintain tractable training and inference. By


00087 [PDF p.2] drawing insights from both Bayes Filters and RNNs we develop a novel hybrid model, Predictive


00088 [PDF p.2] State Recurrent Neural Networks (PSRNNs). Like many successful RNN architectures, PSRNNs


00089 [PDF p.2] use (potentially deeply composed) bilinear transfer functions to combine information from multiple


00090 [PDF p.2] sources. We show that such bilinear functions arise naturally from state updates in Bayes filters like


00091 [PDF p.2] PSRs, in which observations can be viewed as gating belief states. We show that PSRNNs directly


00092 [PDF p.2] generalize discrete PSRs, and can be learned effectively by combining Backpropogation Through


00093 [PDF p.2] Time (BPTT) with an approximately consistent method-of-moments initialization called two-stage


00094 [PDF p.2] regression (2SR). We also show that PSRNNs can be factorized using tensor decomposition, reducing


00095 [PDF p.2] model size and suggesting interesting connections to existing multiplicative architectures such as


00096 [PDF p.2] LSTMs. Finally, we note that our initialization for PSRNNs is approximately consistent in the case


00097 [PDF p.2] of discrete data.

00110 [PDF p.2] by training the RNN on this dataset.

00112 [PDF p.2] approach consists of a spectral initialization, followed by fine tuning via BPTT. They show that

00126 [PDF p.3] 3     Background


00127 [PDF p.3] 3.1   Predictive State Representations


00128 [PDF p.3] 


00129 [PDF p.3] Predictive state representations (PSRs) [4] are a class of models for filtering, prediction, and simulation


00130 [PDF p.3] of discrete time dynamical systems. PSRs provide a compact representation of a dynamical system


00131 [PDF p.3] by representing state as a set of predictions of features of future observations.


00132 [PDF p.3] We define a predictive state qt = qt|t−1 = E[ft | ht ], where ft = f (ot:t+k−1 ) is a vector of features


00133 [PDF p.3] of future observations and ht = h(o1:t−1 ) is a vector of features of historical observations. The


00134 [PDF p.3] features are selected such that qt determines the distribution of future observations P (ot:t+k−1 |


00135 [PDF p.3] o1:t−1 ).1 Filtering is the process of mapping a predictive state qt to qt+1 conditioned on ot , while


00136 [PDF p.3] prediction maps a predictive state qt = qt|t−1 to qt+k|t−1 = E[ft+k | o1:t−1 ] without intervening


00137 [PDF p.3] observations.


00138 [PDF p.3] PSRs were originally developed for discrete data as a generalization of existing Bayes Filters such as


00139 [PDF p.3] HMMs [4]. However, by leveraging the recent concept of Hilbert Space embeddings of distributions


00140 [PDF p.3] [24], which can be used to embed the PSR in a Hilbert Space, we can generalize the PSR to continuous


00141 [PDF p.3] observations [8]. Hilbert Space Embeddings of PSRs (HSE-PSRs) [8] represent the state as one


00142 [PDF p.3] or more nonparametric conditional embedding operators in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space


00143 [PDF p.3] (RKHS) [25] and use Kernel Bayes Rule (KBR) [24] to estimate, predict, and update the state.


00144 [PDF p.3] For a full treatment of HSE-PSRs see [8]. Let kf , kh , ko be translation invariant kernels [26] defined


00145 [PDF p.3] on ft , ht , and ot respectively. We use Random Fourier Features [26] (RFF) to define projections


00146 [PDF p.3] φt = RFF (ft ), ηt = RFF (ht ), and ωt = RFF (ot ) such that kf (fi , fj ) = φTi φj , kh (hi , hj ) =


00147 [PDF p.3] ηiT ηj , ko (oi , oj ) = ωiT ωj . Using this notation, the HSE-PSR predictive state is qt = E[φt | ηt ].


00148 [PDF p.3] Formally an HSE-PSR (hereafter simply referred to as a PSR) consists of an initial state b1 , a 3-mode


00149 [PDF p.3] update tensor W , and a 3-mode normalization tensor Z. The PSR update equation is


00150 [PDF p.3]                                                                            −1

00208 [PDF p.4] 4     Predictive State Recurrent Neural Networks


00209 [PDF p.4] In this section we introduce Predictive State Recurrent Neural Networks (PSRNNs), a new RNN


00210 [PDF p.4] architecture inspired by PSRs. PSRNNs allow for a principled initialization and refinement via BPTT.


00211 [PDF p.4] The key contributions which led to the development of PSRNNs are: 1) a new normalization scheme


00212 [PDF p.4] for PSRs which allows for effective refinement via BPTT; 2) the extention of the 2SR algorithm to a


00213 [PDF p.4] multilayered architecture; and 3) the optional use of a tensor decomposition to obtain a more scalable


00214 [PDF p.4] model.

00266 [PDF p.5]

00299 [PDF p.5] layer is able to learn to improve on the first layer’s performance.

00337 [PDF p.6] We determined experimentally that factorized PSRNNs are poorly conditioned when compared with


00338 [PDF p.6] PSRNNs, due to very large and very small numbers often occurring in the CP decomposition. To


00339 [PDF p.6] alleviate this issue, we need to initialize the bias b in a factorized PSRNN to be a small multiple of


00340 [PDF p.6] the mean state. This acts to stabilize the model, regularizing gradients and preventing us from moving


00341 [PDF p.6] away from the good local optimum provided by 2SR.


00342 [PDF p.6] We note that a similar stabilization happens automatically in randomly initialized RNNs: after the


00343 [PDF p.6] first few iterations the gradient updates cause the biases to become non-zero, stabilizing the model


00344 [PDF p.6] and resulting in subsequent gradient descent updates being reasonable. Initialization of the biases is


00345 [PDF p.6] only a concern for us because we do not want the original model to move away from our carefully


00346 [PDF p.6] prepared initialization due to extreme gradients during the first few steps of gradient descent.


00347 [PDF p.6] In summary, we can learn factorized PSRNNs by first using 2SR to initialize a PSRNN, then using CP


00348 [PDF p.6] decomposition to factorize the tensor model parameters to obtain a factorized PSRNN, then applying


00349 [PDF p.6] BPTT to the refine the factorized PSRNN.

00351 [PDF p.6] 5     Discussion


00352 [PDF p.6] The value of bilinear units in RNNs was the focus of recent work by Wu et al [33]. They introduced the


00353 [PDF p.6] concept of Multiplicative Integration (MI) units — components of the form Ax By — and showed


00354 [PDF p.6] that replacing additive units by multiplicative ones in a range of architectures leads to significantly


00355 [PDF p.6] improved performance. As Eq. (8) shows, factorizing W leads precisely to an architecture with MI


00356 [PDF p.6] units.


00357 [PDF p.6] 


00358 [PDF p.6] 


00359 [PDF p.6]                                                     6


00360 [PDF p.6] 


00361 [PDF p.7] Modern RNN architectures such as LSTMs and GRUs are known to outperform traditional RNN


00362 [PDF p.7] architectures on many problems [12]. While the success of these methods is not fully understood,


00363 [PDF p.7] much of it is attributed to the fact that these architectures possess a gating mechanism which allows


00364 [PDF p.7] them both to remember information for a long time, and also to forget it quickly. Crucially, we note


00365 [PDF p.7] that PSRNNs also allow for a gating mechanism. To see this consider a single entry in the factorized


00366 [PDF p.7] PSRNN update (omitting normalization).

00372 [PDF p.7] The current state qt will only contribute to the new state if the function k Bjk [ot ]k of ot is non-zero.


00373 [PDF p.7] Otherwise ot will cause the model to forget this information: the bilinear component of the PSRNN

00375 [PDF p.7] We note that similar bilinear forms occur as components of many successful models. For example,


00376 [PDF p.7] consider the (one layer) GRU update equation:

00380 [PDF p.7] The GRU update is a convex combination of the existing state qt and and update term Wh ot +Uh (rt


00381 [PDF p.7] qt ) + ch . We see that the core part of this update term Uh (rt qt ) + ch bears a striking similarity to


00382 [PDF p.7] our factorized PSRNN update. The PSRNN update is simpler, though, since it omits the nonlinearity


00383 [PDF p.7] σ(·), and hence is able to combine pairs of linear updates inside and outside σ(·) into a single matrix.


00384 [PDF p.7] Finally, we would like to highlight the fact that, as discussed in section 5, the bilinear form shared in


00385 [PDF p.7] some form by these models (including PSRNNs) resembles the first component of the Kernel Bayes


00386 [PDF p.7] Rule update function. This observation suggests that bilinear components are a natural structure to


00387 [PDF p.7] use when constructing RNNs, and may help explain the success of the above methods over alternative


00388 [PDF p.7] approaches. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that there are no activation functions (other than


00389 [PDF p.7] divisive normalization) present in our PSRNN architecture, yet it still manages to achieve strong


00390 [PDF p.7] performance.

00503 [PDF p.9] 8   Conclusions


00504 [PDF p.9] We present PSRNNs: a new approach for modelling time-series data that hybridizes Bayes filters with


00505 [PDF p.9] RNNs. PSRNNs have both a principled initialization procedure and a rich functional form. The basic


00506 [PDF p.9] PSRNN block consists of a 3-mode tensor, corresponding to bilinear combination of the state and


00507 [PDF p.9] observation, followed by divisive normalization. These blocks can be arranged in layers to increase


00508 [PDF p.9] the expressive power of the model. We showed that tensor CP decomposition can be used to obtain


00509 [PDF p.9] 


00510 [PDF p.9] 


00511 [PDF p.9]                                                       9


00512 [PDF p.9] 


00513 [PDF p.10] factorized PSRNNs, which allow flexibly selecting the number of states and model parameters. We


00514 [PDF p.10] showed how factorized PSRNNs can be viewed as both an instance of Kernel Bayes Rule and a gated


00515 [PDF p.10] architecture, and discussed links to existing multiplicative architectures such as LSTMs. We applied


00516 [PDF p.10] PSRNNs to 4 datasets and showed that we outperform alternative approaches in all cases.

00633 [PDF p.14] A    On the Consistency of Initialization


00634 [PDF p.14] In this section we provide a theoretical justification for the PSRNN. Specifically, we show that in


00635 [PDF p.14] the case of discrete observations and a single layer the PSRNN provides a good approximation to a


00636 [PDF p.14] consistent model. We first show that in the discrete setting using a matrix inverse is equivalent to a


00637 [PDF p.14] sum normalization. We subsequently show that, under certain conditions, two-norm normalization


00638 [PDF p.14] has the same effect as sum-normalization. .


00639 [PDF p.14] Let qt be the PSR state at time t, and ot be the observation at time t (as an indicator vector). In


00640 [PDF p.14] this setting the covariance matrix Ct = E[ot × ot |o1:t−1 ] will be diagonal. By assumption, the
