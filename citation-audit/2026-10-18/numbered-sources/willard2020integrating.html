00223 [PDF p.5] 2.1   Improving over state-of-the-art physical models


00224 [PDF p.5] First-principle models are used extensively in a wide range of engineering and environmental


00225 [PDF p.5] applications. Even though these models are based on known physical laws, in most cases, they


00226 [PDF p.5] are necessarily approximations of reality due to incomplete knowledge of certain processes, which


00227 [PDF p.5] introduces bias. In addition, they often contain a large number of parameters whose values must be


00228 [PDF p.5] estimated with the help of limited observed data, degrading their performance further, especially due


00229 [PDF p.5] to heterogeneity in the underlying processes in both space and time. The limitations of physics-based


00230 [PDF p.5] models cut across discipline boundaries and are well known in the scientific community (e.g., see


00231 [PDF p.5] Gupta et al. [116] in the context of hydrology).


00232 [PDF p.5]    ML models have been shown to outperform physics-based models in many disciplines (e.g.,


00233 [PDF p.5] materials science [148, 238, 285], applied physics [22, 127], aquatic sciences [132, 286], atmospheric


00234 [PDF p.5] science [206], biomedical science [264], computational biology [9]). A major reason for this success


00235 [PDF p.5] is that ML models (e.g., neural networks), given enough data, can find structure and patterns in


00236 [PDF p.5] problems where complexity prohibits the explicit programming of a system’s exact physical nature.


00237 [PDF p.5] Given this ability to automatically extract complex relationships from data, ML models appear


00238 [PDF p.5] promising for scientific problems with physical processes that are not fully understood but have data


00239 [PDF p.5] of adequate quality and quantity is available. However, the black-box application of ML has met with


00240 [PDF p.5] limited success in scientific domains due to a number of reasons [141]: (i) while state-of-the-art ML


00241 [PDF p.5] models are capable of capturing complex spatiotemporal relationships, they require far too much


00242 [PDF p.5] labeled data for training, which is rarely available in real application settings, (ii) ML models often


00243 [PDF p.5] produce scientifically inconsistent results; and (iii) ML models can only capture relationships in


00244 [PDF p.5] the available training data, and thus cannot generalize to out-of-sample scenarios (i.e., those not


00245 [PDF p.5] represented in the training data).


00246 [PDF p.5]    The key objective here is to combine elements of physics-based modeling with state-of-the-art


00247 [PDF p.5] ML models to leverage their complementary strengths. Such integrated physics-ML models are


00248 [PDF p.5] expected to better capture the dynamics of scientific systems and advance the understanding of


00249 [PDF p.5] underlying physical processes. An early attempt for combining ML with physics-based modeling


00250 [PDF p.5] in lake temperature dynamics [231]) has already demonstrated its potential for providing better


00251 [PDF p.5] prediction accuracy with a much smaller number of samples as well as generalizability in out-of-


00252 [PDF p.5] sample scenarios.

00254 [PDF p.5] 2.2   Downscaling


00255 [PDF p.5] Complex mechanistic models are capable of capturing physical reality more precisely than simpler


00256 [PDF p.5] models, as they often involve more diverse components that account for a greater number of processes


00257 [PDF p.5] at finer spatial or temporal resolution. However, given the computation cost and modeling complexity,


00258 [PDF p.5] many models are run at a resolution that is coarser than what is required to precisely capture


00259 [PDF p.5] underlying physical processes. For example, cloud-resolving models (CRM) need to run at sub-


00260 [PDF p.5] kilometer horizontal resolution to be able to effectively represent boundary-layer eddies and low


00261 [PDF p.5] clouds, which are crucial for the modeling of Earth’s energy balance and the cloud–radiation


00262 [PDF p.5] feedback [230]. However, it is not feasible to run global climate models at such fine resolutions even


00263 [PDF p.5] with the most powerful computers expected to be available in the near future.


00264 [PDF p.5]    Downscaling techniques have been widely used as a solution to capture physical variables that


00265 [PDF p.5] need to be modeled at a finer resolution. In general, the downscaling techniques can be divided into


00266 [PDF p.5] two categories: statistical downscaling and dynamical downscaling. Statistical downscaling refers to


00267 [PDF p.5] the use of empirical models to predict finer-resolution variables from coarser-resolution variables.


00268 [PDF p.5] Such a mapping across different resolutions can involve complex non-linear relationships that cannot


00269 [PDF p.5] be precisely modeled by traditional empirical models. Recently, artificial neural networks have shown

00286 [PDF p.6] 2.3    Parameterization


00287 [PDF p.6] Complex physics-based models (e.g., for simulating phenomena in climate, weather, turbulence


00288 [PDF p.6] modeling, hydrology) often use an approach known as parameterization to account for missing


00289 [PDF p.6] physics. In parameterization (note that this term has a different meaning when used in mathematics


00290 [PDF p.6] and geometry), specific complex dynamical processes are replaced by simplified physical approx-


00291 [PDF p.6] imations that are represented as static parameters. A common way to estimate the value of these


00292 [PDF p.6] parameters is to use grid search over the space of combinations of parameter values that lead to the


00293 [PDF p.6] best match with observations. This procedure is referred to as parameter calibration. The failure to


00294 [PDF p.6] correctly parameterize can make the model less robust, and errors that result from imperfect para-


00295 [PDF p.6] meterization can also feed into other components of the entire physics-based model and deteriorate


00296 [PDF p.6] the modeling of important physical processes. Another way, that is being considered increasingly, is


00297 [PDF p.6] to replace processes that are too complex to be physically represented in the model by a simplified


00298 [PDF p.6] dynamic or statistical/ML process. This makes it possible to learn new parameterizations directly


00299 [PDF p.6] from observations and/or high-resolution model simulation using ML methods. Already, ML-based


00300 [PDF p.6] parameterizations have shown success in geology [52, 109], atmospheric science [40, 103], and


00301 [PDF p.6] hydrology [29]. A major benefit of ML-based parameterizations is the reduction of computation


00302 [PDF p.6] time compared to traditional physics-based simulations. In chemistry, Hansen et al. [120] find that


00303 [PDF p.6] parameterizations of atomic energy using ML take seconds compared to multiple days for more


00304 [PDF p.6] standard quantum-calculation calculations, and Behler et al. [27] find that neural networks can vastly


00305 [PDF p.6] improve the efficiency of finding potential energy surfaces of molecules.


00306 [PDF p.6]    Most of the existing work uses standard black box ML models for parameterization, but there is


00307 [PDF p.6] an increasing interest in integrating physics in the ML models [32], as it has the potential to make


00308 [PDF p.6] them more robust and generalizable to unseen scenarios as well as reduce the number of training


00309 [PDF p.6] samples needed for training.

00311 [PDF p.6] 2.4    Reduced-Order Models


00312 [PDF p.6] Reduced-Order Models (ROMs) are computationally inexpensive representations of more complex


00313 [PDF p.6] models. Usually, constructing ROMs involves dimensionality reduction that attempts to capture


00314 [PDF p.6] the most important dynamical characteristics of often large, high-fidelity simulations and models


00315 [PDF p.6] of physical systems (e.g., in fluid dynamics [164]). This can also be viewed as a controlled loss


00316 [PDF p.6] of accuracy. A common way to do this is to project the governing equations of a system onto a


00317 [PDF p.6] linear subspace of the original state space using a method such as principal components analysis


00318 [PDF p.6] or dynamic mode decomposition [221]. However, limiting the dynamics to a lower-dimensional


00319 [PDF p.6] subspace inherently limits the accuracy of any ROM.
