00013 [PDF p.1]          Recently there has been substantial interest in spectral methods for learning dy-


00014 [PDF p.1]          namical systems. These methods are popular since they often offer a good tradeoff


00015 [PDF p.1]          between computational and statistical efficiency. Unfortunately, they can be dif-


00016 [PDF p.1]          ficult to use and extend in practice: e.g., they can make it difficult to incorporate


00017 [PDF p.1]          prior information such as sparsity or structure. To address this problem, we present


00018 [PDF p.1]          a new view of dynamical system learning: we show how to learn dynamical sys-


00019 [PDF p.1]          tems by solving a sequence of ordinary supervised learning problems, thereby


00020 [PDF p.1]          allowing users to incorporate prior knowledge via standard techniques such as


00021 [PDF p.1]          L1 regularization. Many existing spectral methods are special cases of this new


00022 [PDF p.1]          framework, using linear regression as the supervised learner. We demonstrate the


00023 [PDF p.1]          effectiveness of our framework by showing examples where nonlinear regression


00024 [PDF p.1]          or lasso let us learn better state representations than plain linear regression does;


00025 [PDF p.1]          the correctness of these instances follows directly from our general analysis.

00028 [PDF p.1] 1    Introduction


00029 [PDF p.1] 


00030 [PDF p.1] Likelihood-based approaches to learning dynamical systems, such as EM [1] and MCMC [2], can


00031 [PDF p.1] be slow and suffer from local optima. This difficulty has resulted in the development of so-called


00032 [PDF p.1] “spectral algorithms” [3], which rely on factorization of a matrix of observable moments; these


00033 [PDF p.1] algorithms are often fast, simple, and globally optimal.


00034 [PDF p.1] Despite these advantages, spectral algorithms fall short in one important aspect compared to EM and


00035 [PDF p.1] MCMC: the latter two methods are meta-algorithms or frameworks that offer a clear template for


00036 [PDF p.1] developing new instances incorporating various forms of prior knowledge. For spectral algorithms,


00037 [PDF p.1] by contrast, there is no clear template to go from a set of probabilistic assumptions to an algorithm.


00038 [PDF p.1] In fact, researchers often relax model assumptions to make the algorithm design process easier,


00039 [PDF p.1] potentially discarding valuable information in the process.


00040 [PDF p.1] To address this problem, we propose a new framework for dynamical system learning, using the


00041 [PDF p.1] idea of instrumental-variable regression [4, 5] to transform dynamical system learning to a sequence


00042 [PDF p.1] of ordinary supervised learning problems. This transformation allows us to apply the rich literature


00043 [PDF p.1] on supervised learning to incorporate many types of prior knowledge. Our new methods subsume a


00044 [PDF p.1] variety of existing spectral algorithms as special cases.


00045 [PDF p.1] The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: first we formulate the new learning framework


00046 [PDF p.1] (Sec. 2). We then provide theoretical guarantees for the proposed methods (Sec. 4). Finally, we give

00088 [PDF p.2] 2     A framework for spectral algorithms


00089 [PDF p.2] 


00090 [PDF p.2] A dynamical system is a stochastic process (i.e., a distribution over sequences of observations) such


00091 [PDF p.2] that, at any time, the distribution of future observations is fully determined by a vector st called the


00092 [PDF p.2] latent state. The process is specified by three distributions: the initial state distribution P (s1 ), the


00093 [PDF p.2] state transition distribution P (st+1 | st ), and the observation distribution P (ot | st ). For later use,


00094 [PDF p.2] we write the observation ot as a function of the state st and random noise t , as shown in Figure 1.


00095 [PDF p.2] Given a dynamical system, one of the fundamental tasks is to perform inference, where we predict


00096 [PDF p.2] future observations given a history of observations. Typically this is accomplished by maintaining


00097 [PDF p.2] a distribution or belief over states bt|t−1 = P (st | o1:t−1 ) where o1:t−1 denotes the first t − 1


00098 [PDF p.2] observations. bt|t−1 represents both our knowledge and our uncertainty about the true state of the


00099 [PDF p.2] system. Two core inference tasks are filtering and prediction.1 In filtering, given the current belief


00100 [PDF p.2] bt = bt|t−1 and a new observation ot , we calculate an updated belief bt+1 = bt+1|t that incorporates


00101 [PDF p.2] ot . In prediction, we project our belief into the future: given a belief bt|t−1 we estimate bt+k|t−1 =


00102 [PDF p.2] P (st+k | o1:t−1 ) for some k > 0 (without incorporating any intervening observations).


00103 [PDF p.2] The typical approach for learning a dynamical system is to explicitly learn the initial, transition, and


00104 [PDF p.2] observation distributions by maximum likelihood. Spectral algorithms offer an alternate approach


00105 [PDF p.2] to learning: they instead use the method of moments to set up a system of equations that can be


00106 [PDF p.2] solved in closed form to recover estimates of the desired parameters. In this process, they typically


00107 [PDF p.2] factorize a matrix or tensor of observed moments—hence the name “spectral.”


00108 [PDF p.2] Spectral algorithms often (but not always [6]) avoid explicitly estimating the latent state or the initial,


00109 [PDF p.2] transition, or observation distributions; instead they recover observable operators that can be used


00110 [PDF p.2] to perform filtering and prediction directly. To do so, they use an observable representation: instead


00111 [PDF p.2] of maintaining a belief bt over states st , they maintain the expected value of a sufficient statistic of


00112 [PDF p.2] future observations. Such a representation is often called a (transformed) predictive state [7].


00113 [PDF p.2] In more detail, we define qt = qt|t−1 = E[ψt | o1:t−1 ], where ψt = ψ(ot:t+k−1 ) is a vector of future


00114 [PDF p.2] features. The features are chosen such that qt determines the distribution of future observations

00126 [PDF p.3] A typical way to derive a spectral method is to select a set of moments involving ψt , work out the


00127 [PDF p.3] expected values of these moments in terms of the observable operators, then invert this relationship


00128 [PDF p.3] to get an equation for the observable operators in terms of the moments. We can then plug in an


00129 [PDF p.3] empirical estimate of the moments to compute estimates of the observable operators.


00130 [PDF p.3] While effective, this approach can be statistically inefficient (the goal of being able to solve for the


00131 [PDF p.3] observable operators is in conflict with the goal of maximizing statistical efficiency) and can make


00132 [PDF p.3] it difficult to incorporate prior information (each new source of information leads to new moments


00133 [PDF p.3] and a different and possibly harder set of equations to solve). To address these problems, we show


00134 [PDF p.3] that we can instead learn the observable operators by solving three supervised learning problems.


00135 [PDF p.3] The main idea is that, just as we can represent a belief about a latent state st as the conditional


00136 [PDF p.3] expectation of a vector of observable statistics, we can also represent any other distributions needed


00137 [PDF p.3] for prediction and filtering via their own vectors of observable statistics. Given such a representation,


00138 [PDF p.3] we can learn to filter and predict by learning how to map these vectors to one another.


00139 [PDF p.3] In particular, the key intermediate quantity for filtering is the “extended and marginalized” belief


00140 [PDF p.3] P (ot , st+1 | o1:t−1 )—or equivalently P (ot:t+k | o1:t−1 ). We represent this distribution via a vector


00141 [PDF p.3] ξt = ξ(ot:t+k ) of features of the extended future. The features are chosen such that the extended


00142 [PDF p.3] state pt = E[ξt | o1:t−1 ] determines P (ot:t+k | o1:t−1 ). Given P (ot:t+k | o1:t−1 ), filtering and


00143 [PDF p.3] prediction reduce respectively to conditioning on and marginalizing over ot .


00144 [PDF p.3] In many models (including Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and Kalman filters), the extended state


00145 [PDF p.3] pt is linearly related to the predictive state qt —a property we exploit for our framework. That is,


00146 [PDF p.3]                                                  pt = W qt                                                 (1)


00147 [PDF p.3] for some linear operator W . For example, in a discrete system ψt can be an indicator vector repre-


00148 [PDF p.3] senting the joint assignment of the next k observations, and ξt can be an indicator vector for the next


00149 [PDF p.3] k + 1 observations. The matrix W is then the conditional probability table P (ot:t+k | ot:t+k−1 ).


00150 [PDF p.3] Our goal, therefore, is to learn this mapping W . Naı̈vely, we might try to use linear regression for


00151 [PDF p.3] this purpose, substituting samples of ψt and ξt in place of qt and pt since we cannot observe qt or


00152 [PDF p.3] pt directly. Unfortunately, due to the overlap between observation windows, the noise terms on ψt


00153 [PDF p.3] and ξt are correlated. So, naı̈ve linear regression will give a biased estimate of W .


00154 [PDF p.3] To counteract this bias, we employ instrumental regression [4, 5]. Instrumental regression uses in-


00155 [PDF p.3] strumental variables that are correlated with the input qt but not with the noise t:t+k . This property


00156 [PDF p.3] provides a criterion to denoise the inputs and outputs of the original regression problem: we remove


00157 [PDF p.3] that part of the input/output that is not correlated with the instrumental variables. In our case, since


00158 [PDF p.3] past observations o1:t−1 do not overlap with future or extended future windows, they are not cor-


00159 [PDF p.3] related with the noise t:t+k+1 , as can be seen in Figure 1. Therefore, we can use history features


00160 [PDF p.3] ht = h(o1:t−1 ) as instrumental variables.

00248 [PDF p.5] 3   Related Work


00249 [PDF p.5] This work extends predictive state learning algorithms for dynamical systems, which include spec-


00250 [PDF p.5] tral algorithms for Kalman filters [11], Hidden Markov Models [3, 12], Predictive State Represen-


00251 [PDF p.5] tations (PSRs) [13, 14] and Weighted Automata [15]. It also extends kernel variants such as [9],


00252 [PDF p.5] which builds on [16]. All of the above work effectively uses linear regression or linear ridge regres-


00253 [PDF p.5] sion (although not always in an obvious way).


00254 [PDF p.5] One common aspect of predictive state learning algorithms is that they exploit the covariance struc-


00255 [PDF p.5] ture between future and past observation sequences to obtain an unbiased observable state represen-


00256 [PDF p.5] tation. Boots and Gordon [17] note the connection between this covariance and (linear) instrumental


00257 [PDF p.5] regression in the context of the HSE-HMM. We use this connection to build a general framework for


00258 [PDF p.5] dynamical system learning where the state space can be identified using arbitrary (possibly nonlin-


00259 [PDF p.5] ear) supervised learning methods. This generalization lets us incorporate prior knowledge to learn


00260 [PDF p.5] compact or regularized models; our experiments demonstrate that this flexibility lets us take better


00261 [PDF p.5] advantage of limited data.


00262 [PDF p.5] Reducing the problem of learning dynamical systems with latent state to supervised learning bears


00263 [PDF p.5] similarity to Langford et al.’s sufficient posterior representation (SPR) [18], which encodes the state


00264 [PDF p.5] by the sufficient statistics of the conditional distribution of the next observation and represents sys-


00265 [PDF p.5] tem dynamics by three vector-valued functions that are estimated using supervised learning ap-


00266 [PDF p.5] proaches. While SPR allows all of these functions to be non-linear, it involves a rather complicated


00267 [PDF p.5] training procedure involving multiple iterations of model refinement and model averaging, whereas


00268 [PDF p.5] our framework only requires solving three regression problems in sequence. In addition, the theo-


00269 [PDF p.5] retical analysis of [18] only establishes the consistency of SPR learning assuming that all regression


00270 [PDF p.5] steps are solved perfectly. Our work, on the other hand, establishes convergence rates based on the


00271 [PDF p.5] performance of S1 regression.

00395 [PDF p.7] 5.1.2        Models and Evaluation


00396 [PDF p.7] 


00397 [PDF p.7] Under the (reasonable) assumption that the two states have distinct observation probabilities, this


00398 [PDF p.7] model is 1-observable. Hence we define the predictive state to be the expected next observation,


00399 [PDF p.7] which results in the following statistics: ψt = ot and ξt = ot ⊗k ot+1 , where ot is represented by


00400 [PDF p.7] a 2 dimensional indicator vector and ⊗k denotes the Kronecker product. Given these statistics, the


00401 [PDF p.7] extended state pt = E[ξt | o1:t−1 ] is a joint probability table of ot:t+1 .


00402 [PDF p.7] We compare three models that differ by history features and S1 regression method:


00403 [PDF p.7] Spec-HMM: This baseline uses ht = ot−1 and linear S1 regression, making it equivalent to the


00404 [PDF p.7] spectral HMM method of [3], as detailed in the supplementary material.


00405 [PDF p.7] Feat-HMM: This baseline represents ht by an indicator vector of the joint assignment of the previ-


00406 [PDF p.7] ous b observations (we set b to 4) and uses linear S1 regression. This is essentially a feature-based


00407 [PDF p.7] spectral HMM [12]. It thus incorporates more history information compared to Spec-HMM at the


00408 [PDF p.7] expense of increasing the number of S1 parameters by O(2b ).


00409 [PDF p.7] LR-HMM: This model represents ht by a binary vector of length b encoding the previous b obser-


00410 [PDF p.7] vations and uses logistic regression as the S1 model. Thus, it uses the same history information as


00411 [PDF p.7] Feat-HMM but reduces the number of parameters to O(b) at the expense of inductive bias.


00412 [PDF p.7] We evaluated the above models using 1000 random splits of the 325 sequences into 200 training


00413 [PDF p.7] and 125 testing. For each testing observation ot we compute the absolute error between actual and


00414 [PDF p.7] expected value (i.e. |δot =1 − P̂ (ot = 1 | o1:t−1 )|). We report the mean absolute error for each split.


00415 [PDF p.7] The results are displayed in Figure 4.6 We see that, while incorporating more history information


00416 [PDF p.7] increases accuracy (Feat-HMM vs. Spec-HMM), being able to incorporate the same information


00417 [PDF p.7] using a more compact model gives an additional gain in accuracy (LR-HMM vs. Feat-HMM). We


00418 [PDF p.7] also compared the LR-HMM method to an HMM trained using expectation maximization (EM). We

00489 [PDF p.8] 6   Conclusion


00490 [PDF p.8] In this work we developed a general framework for dynamical system learning using supervised


00491 [PDF p.8] learning methods. The framework relies on two key principles: first, we extend the idea of predictive


00492 [PDF p.8] state to include extended state as well, allowing us to represent all of inference in terms of predictions


00493 [PDF p.8] of observable features. Second, we use past features as instruments in an instrumental regression,


00494 [PDF p.8] denoising state estimates that then serve as training examples to estimate system dynamics.


00495 [PDF p.8] We have shown that this framework encompasses and provides a unified view of some previous


00496 [PDF p.8] successful dynamical system learning algorithms. We have also demostrated that it can be used


00497 [PDF p.8] to extend existing algorithms to incorporate nonlinearity and regularizers, resulting in better state


00498 [PDF p.8] estimates. As future work, we would like to apply this framework to leverage additional techniques


00499 [PDF p.8] such as manifold embedding and transfer learning in stage 1 regression. We would also like to


00500 [PDF p.8] extend the framework to controlled processes.
