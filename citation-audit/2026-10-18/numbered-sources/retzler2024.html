00093 [PDF p.2]             Abstract


00094 [PDF p.2]             In a Model Predictive Control (MPC) setting, the precise simulation of the behavior of the system over a finite time


00095 [PDF p.2]             window is essential. This application-oriented benchmark study focuses on a robot arm that exhibits various nonlinear


00096 [PDF p.2]             behaviors. For this arm, we have a physics-based model with approximate parameter values and an open benchmark


00097 [PDF p.2]             dataset for system identification. However, the long-term simulation of this model quickly diverges from the actual


00098 [PDF p.2]             arm’s measurements, indicating its inaccuracy. We compare the accuracy of black-box and purely physics-based


00099 [PDF p.2]             approaches with several physics-informed approaches. These involve different combinations of a neural network’s


00100 [PDF p.2]             output with information from the physics-based model or feeding the physics-based model’s information into the


00101 [PDF p.2]             neural network. One of the physics-informed model structures can improve accuracy over a fully black-box model.

00106 [PDF p.2]                 Impact Statement


00107 [PDF p.2]                 Model Predictive Control (MPC) is one of the most impactful and promising technologies in the field of control.


00108 [PDF p.2]                 With the methods compared in this paper, it is possible to create a more accurate forward dynamics model by


00109 [PDF p.2]                 augmenting physics-based models with neural networks, from which MPC will benefit. On the example of a


00110 [PDF p.2]                 robot model corresponding to a challenging nonlinear system identification benchmark dataset, we present


00111 [PDF p.2]                 multiple hybrid model structures, combining physics-informed and data-driven models. This model collection


00112 [PDF p.2]                 equips the users with different exploratory opportunities and gives them additional insight for more educated


00113 [PDF p.2]                 choices on the model. Furthermore, our results confirm that using as much structural insight about the system as


00114 [PDF p.2]                 possible is important.

00133 [PDF p.3]             knowledge to build those models. Practitioners of machine learning, a field that has roots in computer


00134 [PDF p.3]             science, typically focus on huge datasets and use universal approximators like artificial neural networks

00136 [PDF p.3]                In this research, we aimed to create a high-fidelity simulation model of a robot arm for Model


00137 [PDF p.3]             Predictive Control (MPC), as a more accurate model often leads to better tracking performance and a


00138 [PDF p.3]             solution in fewer iterations. All aspects of a system’s behavior cannot be perfectly modeled using


00139 [PDF p.3]             physical laws alone because of the measurement noise and those more complex nonlinear behaviors


00140 [PDF p.3]             that are infeasible to model manually. However, we can still refine our model through machine-


00141 [PDF p.3]             learning techniques that allow us to capture and predict these missing complex behaviors based on the


00142 [PDF p.3]             data. In our case, we require an accurate prediction of the movement of the arm for given actuator


00143 [PDF p.3]             torque inputs over a given finite time window ahead. In robotic MPC applications, this window is


00144 [PDF p.3]             typically between 100 milliseconds (e.g., Zhao et al., 2023) and 1 second (e.g., Trimble et al., 2020).


00145 [PDF p.3]             An overview of the connection between MPC and system identification for our use case is presented


00146 [PDF p.3]             in Figure 1.


00147 [PDF p.3]                To achieve the highest possible accuracy, we evaluate a set of physics-based models augmented with


00148 [PDF p.3]             ANNs in different ways. In general, physics-only models are fully explainable through the physical


00149 [PDF p.3]             relations that govern the system, though they usually provide a lower accuracy. ANN-based models can


00150 [PDF p.3]             often fit very well to the data but fail to extrapolate well on data in a range unseen during training


00151 [PDF p.3]             (as described, for example, in Zhang and Cross, 2022). Furthermore, ANNs are rarely physically


00152 [PDF p.3]             explainable. This lack of generalization calls for combinations of ANN and physics-based models, from


00153 [PDF p.3]             which we expect better accuracy compared to a physics-only model and better explainability and


00154 [PDF p.3]             extrapolation capability compared to an ANN-only model. Out of these metrics, the accuracy of


00155 [PDF p.3]             simulations within a given time window ahead is evaluated in this work, as it is the most relevant to


00156 [PDF p.3]             MPC. Given the computational complexity of calculating these N-step-ahead predictions, one of our


00157 [PDF p.3]             research questions is if we can avoid calculating them during the training and validation steps. Instead, we


00158 [PDF p.3]             use an indirect metric that is easier to compute, but our target is that the final model still reaches a good


00159 [PDF p.3]             result on the N-step-ahead predictions.

00178 [PDF p.4]             1.1. Related work and background


00179 [PDF p.4]             Models of electromechanical systems for prediction over a finite time window have been a long-standing


00180 [PDF p.4]             research focus. Based on physical interpretability, Schoukens and Ljung (2019) define several shades for


00181 [PDF p.4]             model structures, from pit-black to snow-white models, with snow-white being the completely theory-


00182 [PDF p.4]             and knowledge-based model. In the realm of machine learning, the different physics-informed approaches


00183 [PDF p.4]             to deep learning are on various points of this scale. Lutter (2021) introduces a Differentiable Newton–


00184 [PDF p.4]             Euler Algorithm (DiffNEA) that uses virtual parameters to keep all the physical parameters physically


00185 [PDF p.4]             interpretable, combined with gradient-based optimization techniques typically used for machine learning.


00186 [PDF p.4]             DiffNEA results in a wholly physical model that can be integrated with machine-learning approaches like


00187 [PDF p.4]             reinforcement learning, keeping close to white-box approaches on the scale mentioned. It provides


00188 [PDF p.4]             considerably good extrapolation capability and achieves physical interpretability through virtual param-


00189 [PDF p.4]             eters. Some of its friction models are related but, at specific points, different from the combined models in


00190 [PDF p.4]             this work. Deep Lagrangian Networks (DeLaN), also introduced by Lutter (2021), combine deep learning


00191 [PDF p.4]             with Lagrangian mechanics to learn dynamical models that conserve energy, with no specific knowledge


00192 [PDF p.4]             of the system. However, they are close to the black-box endpoint of the scale.


00193 [PDF p.4]                 The general framework of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINN) introduced by Raissi et al. (2019)


00194 [PDF p.4]             features an ANN with a time input that can learn to provide a data-driven solution to partial differential


00195 [PDF p.4]             equations. Extensions to this framework have emerged, with the ANN also having a control input: compared


00196 [PDF p.4]             to the Runge–Kutta-Fehlberg (also known as “RK45”) integration of a physics-based model, Nicodemus


00197 [PDF p.4]             et al. (2022) achieved a reduction in computational time in an MPC setting. Similarly, Lambert et al. (2021)


00198 [PDF p.4]             created long-term simulation models for MPC and reinforcement learning scenarios; both are close to the


00199 [PDF p.4]             complete black-box side of the scale. In contrast to these techniques, our approach is close to the middle of


00200 [PDF p.4]             the interpretability scale, as we calculate part of our model directly from physical relations.


00201 [PDF p.4]                 Another similar approach is called Physics-Guided Neural Networks (PGNN), introduced by Daw


00202 [PDF p.4]             et al. (2022). They apply a physics inconsistency term in the loss function and different combinations of


00203 [PDF p.4]             physics-based models and neural networks, some of which we also evaluate for our application.


00204 [PDF p.4]                 In De Groote et al. (2022), the so-called Neural Network Augmented Physics (NNAP) models were


00205 [PDF p.4]             introduced, where the neural network is inserted into the physics-based model of a slider crank mechanism


00206 [PDF p.4]             to account for unknown nonlinear phenomena (unknown load interaction, effect of spring, and additional


00207 [PDF p.4]             nonlinear friction). Our work shares some similarities with this, though we are exploring and comparing


00208 [PDF p.4]             multiple ways of combining neural networks and physical models.

00211 [PDF p.4]             The system considered is a KUKA KR300 R2500 robot arm with six degrees of freedom, with a 150 kg


00212 [PDF p.4]             mass attached to the end effector (see Figure 2). Its dynamics consist of the interaction of the robot’s rigid


00213 [PDF p.4]             bodies, a contribution from a Hydraulic Weight Counterbalance (HWC) system installed on the second


00214 [PDF p.4]             joint, and nonlinear friction. We expect additional nonlinearities from gearbox deformation and backlash.

00615 [PDF p.12]             2.4. Neural network and optimization details


00616 [PDF p.12]             We use the Adaptive Moment Estimation (ADAM) optimization algorithm of Kingma and Ba (2015),


00617 [PDF p.12]             which has recently become a typical default choice in deep learning, to update the ANN weights or the


00618 [PDF p.12]             physics-based model parameters.


00619 [PDF p.12]                 We use a standard procedure for data partitioning: three different periods as training, validation, and


00620 [PDF p.12]             test datasets. The training set is used to adjust the model parameters in every epoch. The validation set is


00621 [PDF p.12]             also used during training to provide an unbiased model evaluation and mitigate the risk of overfitting. It is


00622 [PDF p.12]             used in early stopping: the optimization is terminated if the validation loss does not demonstrate an


00623 [PDF p.12]             improvement over 1000 epochs while training the ANN. Finally, after the training phase, we use the test


00624 [PDF p.12]             set for an unbiased evaluation of the performance of the final models. The test set also allows us to verify


00625 [PDF p.12]             the extrapolation performance concerning robot poses unseen during training.


00626 [PDF p.12]                 As a note, we break down our training phase into two steps. First, we optimize the physical model


00627 [PDF p.12]             parameters θphy over the course of 5000 epochs, during which only f phy is active (i.e., replaces f fwd in the


00628 [PDF p.12]             objective). Additionally, we disable early stopping to prevent it from being triggered during this period.


00629 [PDF p.12]             Second, we optimize only the ANN parameters θann , and the entire f fwd (with both physical and ANN


00630 [PDF p.12]             parts, when applicable) is active until early stopping is triggered. The first step makes the physical model


00631 [PDF p.12]             more accurate before using its information in the combined models in the second step.


00632 [PDF p.12]                 The data file used was recording_2021_12_15_20H_45M.mat from Weigand et al. (2022),


00633 [PDF p.12]             and the training, validation, and test data are concretely described in Table 3. The software stack we used


00634 [PDF p.12]             to implement this is the deepSI framework from Beintema et al. (2021), which is based on PyTorch.


00635 [PDF p.12]                 The neural network we apply has 1000 neurons per layer, two hidden layers, a residual connection, and


00636 [PDF p.12]             tanh activation function. Models 4 and 5 are exceptions to this, with 100 neurons per layer, but then with


00637 [PDF p.12]             six separate, small networks (one per joint) instead of one large. We apply regularization to the ANN


00638 [PDF p.12]             parameters by the weight decay built into the implementation of the ADAM solver. We applied


00639 [PDF p.12]             hyperparameter tuning to find the right choice for the weight decay. We have been manually tuning the


00640 [PDF p.12]             learning rate of ADAM to find a value that is large enough but does not result in strong fluctuations in the


00641 [PDF p.12]             training loss. This value is 104 for θann and 1:0 for θphy : we apply different learning rates to the two sets of


00642 [PDF p.12]             parameters. Because of these different learning rates for the two components of the model and the need to


00643 [PDF p.12]             balance them well, optimizing θphy and θann together is difficult. We have decided not to optimize them in


00644 [PDF p.12]             the same step. The weight initialization corresponds to the default uniform initialization provided by


00645 [PDF p.12]             deepSI and PyTorch.

00983 [PDF p.16]             5. Conclusions


00984 [PDF p.16]             In this paper, we compared different augmentations of physics-based models with neural networks, using


00985 [PDF p.16]             the example of a robot model corresponding to a challenging nonlinear system identification benchmark


00986 [PDF p.16]             dataset. The presented model collection equips the users with different exploratory opportunities and


00987 [PDF p.16]             gives them additional insight for more educated choices on the model to use to create a more accurate


00988 [PDF p.16]             forward dynamics model for the benefit of MPC.


00989 [PDF p.16]                 In a general MPC application, we suggest using Model 4, because of its low N-step-NRMS error in


00990 [PDF p.16]             Case 2. We have managed to include physical knowledge into this model, as it has a significantly lower


00991 [PDF p.16]             error, 55% of that of the ANN-only Model 0.b. A key to achieving this result was selecting the model


00992 [PDF p.16]             structure based on insight into the system’s physical structure.


00993 [PDF p.16]                 The picture is different if the application targets a region of very low velocities (e.g., very precise


00994 [PDF p.16]             positioning). In Case 2, we see that Model 4 improves upon modeling the sliding friction. In contrast, in


00995 [PDF p.16]             Case 1, we observe that none of the combined models handle the presliding friction (corresponding to low


00996 [PDF p.16]             velocities) particularly well, as neither the physics-based model nor the imposed model structure provides


00997 [PDF p.16]             sufficient information about it.
