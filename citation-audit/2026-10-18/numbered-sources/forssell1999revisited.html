00044 [PDF p.2] Abstract


00045 [PDF p.2] Identi cation of systems operating in closed loop has long been of prime interest


00046 [PDF p.2] in industrial applications. The problem o ers many possibilities, and also some


00047 [PDF p.2] fallacies, and a wide variety of approaches have been suggested, many quite recently.


00048 [PDF p.2] The purpose of the current contribution is to place most of these approaches in a


00049 [PDF p.2] coherent framework, thereby showing their connections and display similarities and


00050 [PDF p.2] di erences in the asymptotic properties of the resulting estimates. The common


00051 [PDF p.2] framework is created by the basic prediction error method, and it is shown that most


00052 [PDF p.2] of the common methods correspond to di erent parameterizations of the dynamics


00053 [PDF p.2] and noise models. The so called indirect methods, e.g., are indeed "direct" methods


00054 [PDF p.2] employing noise models that contain the regulator. The asymptotic properties of


00055 [PDF p.2] the estimates then follow from the general theory and take di erent forms as they


00056 [PDF p.2] are translated to the particular parameterizations. In the course of the analysis we


00057 [PDF p.2] also suggest a projection approach to closed-loop identi cation with the advantage


00058 [PDF p.2] of allowing approximation of the open loop dynamics in a given, and user-chosen


00059 [PDF p.2] frequency domain norm, even in the case of an unknown, non-linear regulator.

00085 [PDF p.3] 1.1 Motivation and Previous Work


00086 [PDF p.3] 


00087 [PDF p.3] System identi cation is a well established eld with a number of approaches,


00088 [PDF p.3] that can broadly be classi ed into the prediction error family, e.g,, [22], the


00089 [PDF p.3] subspace approaches, e.g., [31], and the non-parametric correlation and spectral


00090 [PDF p.3] analysis methods, e.g., [5]. Of special interest is the situation when the data


00091 [PDF p.3] to be used has been collected under closed-loop operation, as in Fig. 1.


00092 [PDF p.3] The fundamental problem with closed-loop data is the correlation between


00093 [PDF p.3] the unmeasurable noise and the input. It is clear that whenever the feedback


00094 [PDF p.3] controller is not identically zero, the input and the noise will be correlated.


00095 [PDF p.3] This is the reason why several methods that work in open loop fail when


00096 [PDF p.3] applied to closed-loop data. This is for example true for the subspace approach


00097 [PDF p.3] and the non-parametric methods, unless special measures are taken. Despite


00098 [PDF p.3] these problems, performing identi cation experiments under output feedback


00099 [PDF p.3] (i.e. in closed loop) may be necessary due to safety or economic reasons, or if


00100 [PDF p.3] the system contains inherent feedback mechanisms. Closed-loop experiments


00101 [PDF p.3] may also be advantageous in certain situations:


00102 [PDF p.3]  In [13] the problem of optimal experiment design is studied. It is shown


00103 [PDF p.3]   that if the model is to be used for minimum variance control design the


00104 [PDF p.3]   identi cation experiment should be performed in closed-loop with the opti-


00105 [PDF p.3]   mal minimum variance controller in the loop. In general it can be seen that


00106 [PDF p.3]   optimal experiment design with variance constraints on the output leads to


00107 [PDF p.3]   closed-loop solutions.


00108 [PDF p.3]  In \identi cation for control" the objective is to achieve a model that is


00109 [PDF p.3]   suited for robust control design (see, e.g., [7, 19, 33]). Thus one has to tailor


00110 [PDF p.3]   the experiment and preprocessing of data so that the model is reliable in


00111 [PDF p.3]   regions where the design process does not tolerate signi cant uncertainties.


00112 [PDF p.3]   The use of closed-loop experiments has been a prominent feature in these


00113 [PDF p.3]   approaches.


00114 [PDF p.3] 


00115 [PDF p.3]                                               3


00116 [PDF p.3] 


00117 [PDF p.4] Historically, there has been a substantial interest in both special identi cation


00118 [PDF p.4] techniques for closed-loop data, and for analysis of existing methods when


00119 [PDF p.4] applied to such data. One of the earliest results was given by Akaike [1] who


00120 [PDF p.4] analyzed the e ect of feedback loops in the system on correlation and spectral


00121 [PDF p.4] analysis. In the seventies there was a very active interest in questions concern-


00122 [PDF p.4] ing closed-loop identi cation, as summarized in the survey paper [15]. See also


00123 [PDF p.4] [3]. Up to this point much of the attention had been directed towards identi -


00124 [PDF p.4] ability and accuracy problems. With the increasing interest "identi cation for


00125 [PDF p.4] control", the focus has shifted to the ability to shape the bias distribution so


00126 [PDF p.4] that control-relevant model approximations of the system are obtained. The


00127 [PDF p.4] surveys [12] and [29] cover most of the results along this line of research.

00129 [PDF p.4] 1.2 Scope and Outline


00130 [PDF p.4] 


00131 [PDF p.4] It is the purpose of the present paper to \revisit" the area of closed-loop


00132 [PDF p.4] identi cation, to put some of the new results and methods into perspective,


00133 [PDF p.4] and to give a status report of what can be done and what cannot. In the course


00134 [PDF p.4] of this expose, some new results will also be generated.


00135 [PDF p.4] We will exclusively deal with methods derived in the prediction error frame-


00136 [PDF p.4] work and most of the results will be given for the multi-input multi-output


00137 [PDF p.4] (MIMO) case. The leading idea in the paper will be to provide a uni ed


00138 [PDF p.4] framework for many closed-loop methods by treating them as di erent pa-


00139 [PDF p.4] rameterizations of the prediction error method:


00140 [PDF p.4]            There is only one method. The di erent approaches are


00141 [PDF p.4]            obtained by di erent parameterizations of the dynamics


00142 [PDF p.4]            and noise models.


00143 [PDF p.4] Despite this we will often use the terminology \method" to distinguish between


00144 [PDF p.4] the di erent approaches and parameterizations. This has also been standard


00145 [PDF p.4] in the literature.


00146 [PDF p.4] The organization of the paper is as follows. Next, in Section 2 we characterize


00147 [PDF p.4] the kinds of assumptions that can be made about the nature of the feedback.


00148 [PDF p.4] This leads to a classi cation of closed-loop identi cation methods into, so


00149 [PDF p.4] called, direct, indirect, and joint input-output methods. As we will show, these


00150 [PDF p.4] approaches can be viewed as variants of the prediction error method with the


00151 [PDF p.4] models parameterized in di erent ways. A consequence of this is that we may


00152 [PDF p.4] use all results for the statistical properties of the prediction error estimates


00153 [PDF p.4] known from the literature. In Section 3 the assumptions we will make regarding


00154 [PDF p.4] the data generating mechanism are formalized. This section also introduces


00155 [PDF p.4] the some of the notation that will be used in the paper.


00156 [PDF p.4] 


00157 [PDF p.4]                                        4

00160 [PDF p.5] well as the basic statements on the asymptotic statistical properties of this


00161 [PDF p.5] method:


00162 [PDF p.5]  Convergence and bias distribution of the limit transfer function estimate.


00163 [PDF p.5]  Asymptotic variance of the transfer function estimates (as the model orders


00164 [PDF p.5]   increase).


00165 [PDF p.5]  Asymptotic variance and distribution of the parameter estimates.

00174 [PDF p.5] 2.1 A Classi cation of Approaches


00175 [PDF p.5] 


00176 [PDF p.5] In the literature several di erent types of closed-loop identi cation methods


00177 [PDF p.5] have been suggested. In general one may distinguish between methods that


00178 [PDF p.5] (a) Assume no knowledge about the nature of the feedback mechanism, and


00179 [PDF p.5]     do not use the reference signal r(t) even if known.


00180 [PDF p.5] (b) Assume the feedback to be known and typically of the form


00181 [PDF p.5]                                  u(t) = r(t) K (q)y(t)                        (1)


00182 [PDF p.5]     where u(t) is the input, y(t) the output, r(t) an external reference signal,


00183 [PDF p.5]     and K (q) a linear time-invariant regulator. The symbol q denotes the


00184 [PDF p.5]     usual shift operator, q y(t) = y(t 1), etc.


00185 [PDF p.5]                              1


00186 [PDF p.5] 


00187 [PDF p.5] (c) Assume the regulator to be unknown, but of a certain structure (like (1)).


00188 [PDF p.5] If the regulator indeed has the form (1), there is no major di erence between


00189 [PDF p.5] (a), (b) and (c): The noise-free relation (1) can be exactly determined based on


00190 [PDF p.5] a fairly short data record, and then r(t) carries no further information about


00191 [PDF p.5] the system, if u(t) is measured. The problem in industrial practice is rather


00192 [PDF p.5] that no regulator has this simple, linear form: Various delimiters, anti-windup


00193 [PDF p.5] functions and other non-linearities will have the input deviate from (1), even


00194 [PDF p.5] if the regulator parameters (e.g. PID-coecients) are known. This strongly


00195 [PDF p.5] disfavors the second approach.


00196 [PDF p.5] In this paper we will use a classi cation of the di erent methods that is similar


00197 [PDF p.5] to the one in [15]. See also [26]. The basis for the classi cation is the di erent


00198 [PDF p.5] 


00199 [PDF p.5]                                         5


00200 [PDF p.5] 


00201 [PDF p.6] kinds of possible assumptions on the feedback listed above. The closed-loop


00202 [PDF p.6] identi cation methods correspondingly fall into the following main groups:


00203 [PDF p.6] (1) The Direct Approach : Ignore the feedback and identify the open-loop


00204 [PDF p.6]     system using measurements of the input u(t) and the output y(t).


00205 [PDF p.6] (2) The Indirect Approach : Identify some closed-loop transfer function and


00206 [PDF p.6]     determine the open-loop parameters using the knowledge of the con-


00207 [PDF p.6]     troller.


00208 [PDF p.6] (3) The Joint Input-Output Approach : Regard the input u(t) and the output


00209 [PDF p.6]     y(t) jointly as the output from a system driven by the reference signal


00210 [PDF p.6]     r(t) and noise. Use some method to determine the open-loop parameters


00211 [PDF p.6]     from an estimate of this system.


00212 [PDF p.6] These categories are basically the same as those in [15], the only di erence


00213 [PDF p.6] is that in the joint input-output approach we allow the joint system to have


00214 [PDF p.6] a measurable input r(t) in addition to the unmeasurable noise e(t). For the


00215 [PDF p.6] indirect approach it can be noted that most methods studied in the literature


00216 [PDF p.6] assume a linear regulator but the same ideas can also be applied if non-linear


00217 [PDF p.6] and/or time-varying controllers are used. The price is, of course, that the


00218 [PDF p.6] estimation problems then become much more involved.


00219 [PDF p.6] In the closed-loop identi cation literature it has been common to classify the


00220 [PDF p.6] methods primarily based on how the nal estimates are computed (e.g. di-


00221 [PDF p.6] rectly or indirectly using multi-step estimation schemes), and then the main


00222 [PDF p.6] groupings have been into \direct" and \indirect" methods. This should not,


00223 [PDF p.6] however, be confused with the classi cation (1)-(3) which is based on the


00224 [PDF p.6] assumptions made on the feedback.

01939 [PDF p.37] 9 Summarizing Discussion


01940 [PDF p.37] 


01941 [PDF p.37] We have attempted to give a status report on identi cation of closed-loop


01942 [PDF p.37] systems. Several methods have been studied and we have shown that most of


01943 [PDF p.37] the common methods can be viewed as special parameterizations of the general


01944 [PDF p.37] prediction error method. This allows simultaneous analysis of the statistical


01945 [PDF p.37] properties of the methods using the general prediction error theory. The main


01946 [PDF p.37] results can be summarized as follows.


01947 [PDF p.37] The direct method


01948 [PDF p.37] 


01949 [PDF p.37]                                         37


01950 [PDF p.37] 


01951 [PDF p.38]  gives consistency and optimal accuracy, regardless of the feedback, if the


01952 [PDF p.38]   noise and dynamic models contain a true description of the system.


01953 [PDF p.38]  requires a correct noise model (possibly estimated) to avoid bias in the dy-


01954 [PDF p.38]   namics transfer function for closed-loop data (Corollary 4). For this reason,


01955 [PDF p.38]   user chosen weightings for custom-made bias distribution cannot be used.


01956 [PDF p.38] The indirect method


01957 [PDF p.38]  requires perfect knowledge of the regulator but can be applied with xed


01958 [PDF p.38]   noise models and still guarantee consistency (Corollary 4).


01959 [PDF p.38] The projection method


01960 [PDF p.38]  allows the true system to be approximated in an arbitrary, user chosen


01961 [PDF p.38]   frequency domain norm regardless of the feedback (Corollary 4).


01962 [PDF p.38]  typically gives worse accuracy than the direct method (Corollary 11).


01963 [PDF p.38] Further comments:


01964 [PDF p.38] (1) The asymptotic variance expressions for increasing model orders are the


01965 [PDF p.38]     same for all methods (Corollary 6).


01966 [PDF p.38] (2) For the nite model order case, the direct method meets the Cramer-Rao


01967 [PDF p.38]     bound and is thus optimal. The indirect method generally gives worse


01968 [PDF p.38]     accuracy. The improved accuracy for the direct method can be traced


01969 [PDF p.38]     to the fact that a constrained noise model (that includes the true noise


01970 [PDF p.38]     description) allows some leverage from the noise part of the input to


01971 [PDF p.38]     estimate the dynamics (Corollary 7 and Lemma 8).


01972 [PDF p.38] The optimal statistical properties, the simplicity, and the general applicabil-


01973 [PDF p.38] ity of the direct method implies that this should be seen as the rst choice


01974 [PDF p.38] of methods for closed-loop identi cation. When this method fails no other


01975 [PDF p.38] method will succeed. In the literature several other methods, e.g., the indirect


01976 [PDF p.38] method, have been advocated mainly due to their ability to shape the bias


01977 [PDF p.38] distribution by use of xed noise models (pre lters). However, it is important


01978 [PDF p.38] to realize that the total error in the resulting estimate will be due to both


01979 [PDF p.38] bias and variance errors and since these methods typically give sub-optimal


01980 [PDF p.38] accuracy, this \advantage" may be questionable.


01981 [PDF p.38] If a low order model is sought that should approximate the system dynamics


01982 [PDF p.38] in a pre-speci ed frequency norm there are two ways to proceed:


01983 [PDF p.38]  First estimate a higher order model, with small bias, using the direct ap-


01984 [PDF p.38]   proach and then reduce this model to lower order using the proper frequency


01985 [PDF p.38]   weighting.


01986 [PDF p.38]  Use the projection method. This has the advantage that a higher order


01987 [PDF p.38]   model need not be estimated, but has the drawback that it may have worse


01988 [PDF p.38] 


01989 [PDF p.38]                                       38


01990 [PDF p.38] 


01991 [PDF p.39]   accuracy.
