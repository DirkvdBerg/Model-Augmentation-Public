# Trajectory Design for Parameter Recovery

**Date**: 2026-04-29
**Status**: Design decisions — T1–T8 implemented in `Matlab-scripts/export_param_recovery.m` (USE_MULTISINE flag); lecture-consistent `generate_multisine` implemented; sampling change and d parameter pending in `train_param_recovery.py`

---

## 1. Context

The parameter recovery training (`train_param_recovery.py`) fits 13 physical parameters of the LPV-LFR gantry model to simulated trajectory data using gradient-based BPTT. The trajectory set must be designed such that every trainable parameter produces a distinguishable, non-collinear effect on the position output. This document records all design decisions made for the trajectory set, including the rationale, the García reference, and planned extensions.

**Trainable parameters (13):**

| Symbol | Description | Units |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| kb1, kb2 | Stiffness of flexible joints 1 and 2 | Nm/rad |
| cg1, cg2 | Viscous friction of actuators X1 and X2 | N/(m/s) |
| cy | Viscous friction of Y-axis | N/(m/s) |
| cb1, cb2 | Viscous friction of flexible joints 1 and 2 | Nm/(rad/s) |
| mh | Mass of payload (Y-axis) — sole LPV scheduling parameter | kg |
| m1, m2 | Mass of actuators X1 and X2 | kg |
| mb | Mass of cross-arm | kg |
| Jb, Jh | Rotary inertia of cross-arm and payload | kg·m² |

**Fixed buffers — not trained:**

| Symbol | Value | Why fixed |
|--------|-------|-----------|
| Lb | 0.725 m | Defines the coordinate transformation Θ = (X1−X2)/Lb — making it trainable contaminates the coordinate definition itself and makes it architecturally invasive throughout the data pipeline. Also precisely known from hardware geometry. |
| d | 0.1 m | CoM offset of payload from cross-arm centre. Currently fixed; **candidate for adding as trainable parameter** — see Section 5. |

**Not modelled (architectural decision):**

Coulomb friction (cc1, cc2, ccy) is absent from the LFR state-space structure — no sign(ẋ) terms. These three parameters are not identified and no trajectory design targets them.

## 2. Reference: García's Identification Experiments

García et al. (2013), Section 2.4, identifies 14 physical parameters via 7 sequential experiments on the physical hardware. Their approach is algebraic and sequential — each experiment zeros out terms so parameters can be read from closed-form expressions. Our gradient-based approach does not need this isolation, but the motion modes García chose are grounded in the natural decoupled coordinates of the system (Y, X, Θ) and carry over directly.

### What is NOT useful from García for our approach

| García element | Reason not applicable |
|---|---|
| Coulomb friction experiments (Exp 1, 3) | Not modelled in LFR |
| Constant-velocity regime design (Exp 1, 3) | Designed to separate viscous from Coulomb algebraically — not needed without Coulomb |
| Constant-acceleration regime design (Exp 2, 4) | Designed for closed-form algebraic recovery — gradient approach handles smooth profiles |
| d identification via F1−F2 asymmetry (Exp 2) | Requires force measurement; currently position output only |
| Static angle hold design (Exp 5) | Zeros out damping and inertia to read stiffness algebraically — not needed for gradient |
| Constant rotational speed design (Exp 6) | Zeros out inertia to read damping algebraically — not needed |
| Bar inertia assumption Eq.(21): Jb=mbLb²/12 | We train Jb and Jh as free parameters — no assumption needed |

### What IS useful from García

The motion modes themselves: Y-axis motion, synchronized X (X1=X2), anti-symmetric X (X1=−X2), and varying Y position for LPV coupling. The specific regime execution (constant velocity, static hold) does not carry over; the mode decomposition does.

## 3. Current Trajectory Set: T1–T6

Generated by `Matlab-scripts/export_param_recovery.m` (formerly `export_lpv_multi_traj.m`). All trajectories use smooth third-order (jerk-limited) set-point profiles. Sampling rate: 20 kHz.

### T1 — Y sweep, conservative

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | Y: 0.3 → −0.3 m |
| vmax_Y | 1.0 m/s |
| amax_Y | 10 m/s² |
| X | stationary |

**Parameters targeted**: cy, mh

**Justification**: At these dynamics, cy·v/(mh·a) = 10×1.0/(10.1×10) = **0.099**. The friction force is ~10% of the inertial force — both cy and mh contribute meaningfully to the position output. This is the friction-dominant Y regime.

**García analog**: Exp 1 (Y constant velocity) + Exp 2 (Y constant acceleration) — combined into one smooth trajectory.

---

### T6 — Y sweep, aggressive

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | Y: 0.3 → −0.3 m |
| vmax_Y | 2.0 m/s (hardware max) |
| amax_Y | 50 m/s² (hardware max) |
| X | stationary |

**Parameters targeted**: mh (primary), cy (secondary)

**Justification**: At hardware-maximum dynamics, cy·v/(mh·a) = 10×2.0/(10.1×50) = **0.040**. The friction force is only 4% of the inertial force — mh dominates. T1 and T6 together provide a 2.5× contrast in the cy/mh ratio. A model with cy too high and mh too low can fit T1 reasonably but will fail on T6, and vice versa. Both trajectories are required to break the cy/mh collinearity. Neither alone is sufficient.

**García analog**: Same as T1.

---

### T2 — X symmetric at Y = 0.3 m

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | X1=X2: 0 → +150 mm |
| vmax_X | 1.5 m/s |
| amax_X | 20 m/s² |
| Y | held at 0.3 m |

**Parameters targeted**: cg1+cg2, m1+m2+mb+mh, mh (via LPV coupling)

**Justification**: Synchronized X motion (X1=X2, Θ≈0) excites the translational X dynamics. At Y=0.3 m, the LPV coupling term M_s[0,1] = (m1−m2)Lb/2 − mh·Y = (m1−m2)·0.3625 − 10.1·0.3 = **−3.03 kg·m** (at nominal, with m1≈m2). This is the maximum coupling in the operating range — mh contributes strongly to the cross-inertia term. The X motion at this Y position makes mh visible in the X equation.

**García analog**: Exp 3 (X synchronized constant velocity) + Exp 4 (X synchronized constant acceleration).

---

### T3 — X symmetric at Y = 0.0 m

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | X1=X2: 0 → +150 mm (identical to T2) |
| vmax_X | 1.5 m/s |
| amax_X | 20 m/s² |
| Y | held at 0.0 m (settle move prepended automatically) |

**Parameters targeted**: cg1+cg2, m1+m2+mb

**Justification**: Identical motion to T2 but at Y=0. The LPV coupling term M_s[0,1] = −mh·0 = **0** — mh does not appear in the X translational equation. The contrast between T2 (mh coupling active) and T3 (mh coupling zero) is the mechanism that breaks the collinearity between mh and (m1+m2+mb). Without T3, mh and the other masses appear only as a sum in the X equations and cannot be separated.

**García analog**: Same as T2, at a different Y position.

---

### T4 — X anti-symmetric at Y = 0.2 m

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | X1: 0 → +35 mm, X2: 0 → −35 mm (differential = 70 mm) |
| vmax_X | 0.5 m/s |
| amax_X | 8 m/s² |
| Y | held at 0.2 m |

**Parameters targeted**: kb1+kb2, cb1+cb2, Jb+Jh

**Justification**: The only trajectory in the set that excites the rotational mode (X1=−X2 → Θ≠0). kb, cb, Jb, Jh appear exclusively in the Θ equation and have zero gradient in every other trajectory regardless of dynamics or multisine excitation. This trajectory is structurally irreplaceable. The 70 mm differential gives Θ = 70/725 = **0.097 rad**, just within the ±0.1 rad mechanical limit. Low dynamics (amax=8 m/s²) chosen to keep Θ within limits given the large restoring torque: kb_sum·Θ = 3975·0.097 = 386 Nm.

**Note**: The current smooth profile barely excites the rotational resonance (~5 Hz, ~32 rad/s). A resonance-targeted multisine on the (F1−F2) channel would directly improve kb/J separation — see Section 6.

**García analog**: Exp 5 (static stiffness) + Exp 6 (constant rotational speed) + Exp 7 (constant rotational acceleration) — all three collapsed into one dynamic trajectory.

---

### T5 — X symmetric + Y sweep simultaneously

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | X1=X2: 0 → +100 mm, Y: 0.2 → −0.2 m (simultaneous) |
| vmax_X | 1.0 m/s |
| amax_X | 15 m/s² |
| vmax_Y | 1.0 m/s |
| amax_Y | 20 m/s² |

**Parameters targeted**: mh over continuous Y range, M1(Y) = −mh·Y schedule

**Justification**: T2 and T3 identify mh at two discrete Y positions (0.3 and 0.0). T5 traces the full LPV coupling schedule by varying Y continuously while X accelerates. This provides gradient signal for mh across the entire scheduling variable range rather than at two points only. This trajectory has no García analog — García's model has no scheduling variable.

## 4. Proposed New Trajectories: T7 and T8

The training loop uses `TRAIN_SEGMENTS_PER_EPOCH = 8`. With 6 trajectories this leaves segments unevenly distributed. Adding 2 trajectories to reach 8 total, with one segment sampled from each trajectory per epoch (see Section 7), gives balanced coverage. The 2 new trajectories are designed to maximise simultaneous parameter excitation — filling the gap that no existing trajectory has all parameters active in the same window.

**Gap in T1–T6**: No trajectory combines anti-symmetric X motion (Θ excitation) with Y motion simultaneously. This means kb, cb, Jb, Jh (only active in T4) and cy, mh, d (only active in Y trajectories) never produce gradient in the same training window.

---

### T7 — X anti-symmetric + Y sweep simultaneously

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | X1: 0 → +35 mm, X2: 0 → −35 mm, Y: 0.3 → −0.3 m (simultaneous) |
| vmax_X | 0.5 m/s |
| amax_X | 8 m/s² |
| vmax_Y | 1.5 m/s |
| amax_Y | 20 m/s² |
| Y_initial | 0.3 m |

**Parameters with non-zero gradient**: all 13 trainable parameters + d

**Justification**: Combines T4's rotational excitation with a full Y sweep. All three DOFs (X translational, Θ rotational, Y payload) are active simultaneously. The critical new capability: Y acceleration while Θ moves creates the mh·d·Ÿ coupling torque — the first trajectory where d is observable from position output (via the resulting Θ perturbation). This is the highest information-density trajectory in the set.

**Physical constraint**: Θ = 70/725 = 0.097 rad — same as T4, within ±0.1 rad limit. The simultaneous Y motion does not increase differential X displacement, so the rotation limit is not additionally stressed.

---

### T8 — X symmetric + X anti-symmetric perturbation + Y sweep

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Motion | X1=X2: 0 → +100 mm (symmetric), X anti perturbation: ±20 mm, Y: 0.2 → −0.2 m |
| vmax_X | 1.0 m/s |
| amax_X | 12 m/s² |
| vmax_Y | 1.5 m/s |
| amax_Y | 25 m/s² |
| Y_initial | 0.2 m |

**Parameters with non-zero gradient**: all 13 trainable parameters + d

**Justification**: Provides a second all-parameters trajectory at a different operating point from T7 — different Y range (0.2→−0.2 vs 0.3→−0.3), different X symmetric/anti-symmetric amplitude ratio, different dynamic levels. Two different all-parameters operating points break remaining collinearities that a single T7 cannot resolve. The X_anti perturbation is kept small (20 mm → Θ = 40/725 = 0.055 rad) to stay well within the rotation limit while still exciting the rotational mode.

## 5. Adding d as a Trainable Parameter

**What d is**: The distance between the centre of mass of the payload and the centre of the cross-arm. Currently fixed at 0.1 m in `lfr_param_block.py` as `_d`.

**Where d appears in the model**:
- Rotary inertia: mh·d² in M_s[1,1]
- Y-to-Θ coupling force: mh·d·Ÿ in the rotational force vector

**Why d is identifiable from position output**: mh appears independently in the Y equation (Eq. 17): mh·Ÿ = Fy − cy·Ẏ. Once mh is identified from Y dynamics, the product mh·d can be separated from the Θ response to Y acceleration. d = (mh·d) / mh. This separation requires a trajectory where Y accelerates while Θ is observed — which T7 provides.

**Why Lb is NOT added for comparison**: Lb defines the coordinate transformation Θ = (X1−X2)/Lb. Making Lb trainable means the coordinate mapping itself becomes a function of a trainable parameter, requiring the full data pipeline to recompute Θ at every gradient step. Additionally, Lb is a precisely known geometric constant measurable with a ruler. d, by contrast, depends on payload mounting and mass distribution and is genuinely uncertain between configurations.

**Implementation**: Add `d` to `_PARAM_NAMES` in `lfr_param_block.py`, initialise with a detuned value, and include it in `_build_matrices` wherever `_d` currently appears as a fixed buffer.

## 6. Multisine Excitation

### 6.1 Injection point

Multisines are injected as **force perturbations on the feedforward channel** (F1, F2, Fy) — not on the position reference. This bypasses the position control loop filtering, directly exciting the mechanical dynamics. The position references remain smooth third-order profiles, so hardware safety constraints on position are not affected by the multisine amplitude.

The Simulink model already has a feedforward input port. The multisine vector (N×3 array: [F_X1, F_X2, F_Y]) is passed as a workspace variable `f` and fed into the model via `setInputs` before calling `sim()`. The existing `export_lpv_multi_traj.m` architecture does this already; `export_param_recovery.m` inherits the same structure.

---

### 6.2 Why multisines, not chirps or PRBS

**Multisine advantages for this application:**

1. **Exact frequency control**: power is placed precisely at chosen frequencies — not smeared across a band. This allows per-trajectory frequency band design (rotational resonance vs broadband).
2. **Crest factor control**: Schroeder phase optimisation keeps peak-to-RMS ratio near 1.5, maximising the usable RMS within hardware saturation limits. A random signal of the same RMS has a crest factor of ~3–4, wasting 6–10 dB of headroom.
3. **Leakage-free by construction**: if the trajectory duration equals an integer number of multisine periods, all excited frequencies sit exactly on DFT bins — no spectral leakage. This is critical for FRF quality.
4. **MIMO design**: different frequency subsets or phase seeds can be assigned to each channel (F1, F2, Fy), making channels distinguishable in the frequency domain. This is impossible with PRBS without frequency-domain post-processing.
5. **Transient handling is tractable**: the transient after the first period can be discarded without wasting the whole record, because the signal is periodic and the transient dies within one or two periods.

**PRBS disadvantage**: white spectrum is good for exploration but gives no crest factor control and no leakage-free property. Not preferred here.

**Chirp disadvantage**: not periodic, so every window segment captures a different frequency sweep — not compatible with the BPTT multi-segment training loop.

---

### 6.3 Strategy: add to all 8 trajectories, filter by physical constraints

Apply multisines to all 8 trajectories and reduce or suppress amplitude where hardware constraints are violated. The amplitude sweep in `export_param_recovery.m` does this automatically — it sweeps over `amp_rms_grid` and accepts the highest amplitude that passes `validate_response`.

**Per-channel frequency band design:**

| Channel | Injected into | f_low (Hz) | f_high (Hz) | Rationale |
|---------|---------------|------------|-------------|-----------|
| Fy | T1, T6, T5, T7, T8 | 1 | 20 | Broadband: cy (velocity-dependent → low-frequency response) and mh (acceleration-dependent → high-frequency response). Frequency contrast separates them without needing two different trajectory regimes. |
| F1+F2 (common mode) | T2, T3, T5, T8 | 1 | 100 | Translational X dynamics have no resonance in this band. Broadband up to 100 Hz excites cg1+cg2 (damping, dominant at higher frequencies) and the translational mass sum (dominant at mid-frequencies). |
| F1−F2 (differential mode) | T4, T7, T8 | 1 | 20 | **Resonance-targeted**: the rotational mode resonance is at ~5 Hz (ω ≈ √(kb_sum/J_eff) ≈ √(3975/3.9) ≈ 32 rad/s). Stiffness kb_sum dominates below resonance; inertia Jb+Jh dominates above. The resonance peak is the most sensitive frequency for their separation. Band capped at 20 Hz — much above resonance, the differential response rolls off and adds no useful information while increasing rotation amplitude risk. |

**Note on off-axis cross-excitation**: Adding Fy to X-only trajectories (T2, T3) or (F1+F2) to Y-only trajectories (T1, T6) creates small cross-axis motion the position controller partially rejects. This is acceptable and beneficial — it produces gradient signal for coupling parameters (mh in the X equation, cg1+cg2 in the Y equation) without requiring the reference trajectory itself to move off-axis.

In `export_param_recovery.m`, all three channels (F_X1, F_X2, F_Y) are always generated. Which channels carry non-zero energy is determined by the trajectory's `ms_f_low`/`ms_f_high` and the MIMO channel design (Section 6.6).

### 6.4 Physical constraint — rotation limit on the (F1−F2) channel

The ±0.1 rad mechanical rotation limit is the tightest constraint in the system.

**T4 base trajectory**: X_anti = 35 mm → differential X = 70 mm → Θ = 70/725 = 0.097 rad. This is within 3% of the limit. A (F1−F2) multisine added on top will produce additional rotation. At frequencies well below resonance (Θ ≈ F_diff / kb_sum) and well above resonance (Θ ≈ F_diff / (J_eff · ω²)); at resonance the response is limited only by damping (Θ_peak ≈ F_diff / (cb_eff · ω_res)). The near-resonance case is the limiting one.

**Concrete problem**: if cb_eff is small (which it may be at nominal parameters), a modest (F1−F2) RMS can produce large rotation amplitude at resonance. The amplitude sweep in `export_param_recovery.m` uses `validate_response` to detect this and cap the amplitude automatically. However, the base trajectory already leaves almost no headroom.

**Resolution options** (in order of preference):

1. **Reduce T4 base amplitude**: set X_anti = 25 mm → Θ_base = 50/725 = 0.069 rad → 31% headroom remains for the multisine. This still adequately excites kb, cb, Jb, Jh while allowing a useful multisine amplitude.
2. **Rely on T7 for resonance-targeted excitation**: T7 has the same X_anti = 35 mm (Θ_base = 0.097 rad), so this does not actually provide more headroom. However, the Y motion in T7 means the rotational dynamics see a different operating trajectory — the amplitude sweep may behave differently.
3. **Accept that T4 gets zero multisine amplitude**: the smooth profile alone still excites the rotational mode. The multisine loss on T4 is not catastrophic — T7 and T8 carry the (F1−F2) broadband excitation.

**Decision pending**: the amplitude sweep outcome will determine which option is required. Document the actual `amp_max` values from the first run with `USE_MULTISINE=true`.

### 6.5 Requirement 1 — Period design and transient handling

**The leakage-free requirement**:

Leakage occurs when the DFT window does not contain an integer number of signal periods. For a multisine with fundamental period T_ms (seconds), the trajectory duration must satisfy:

> T_traj = n_periods × T_ms   (n_periods ≥ 2)

In `generate_multisine`, this means: given N (number of samples) and fs (sample rate), the period length must divide N exactly. The fundamental frequency must therefore be:

> f0 = fs / N   (Hz)

and all excited frequencies must be integer multiples of f0 (harmonic multisine). Non-harmonic multisines relax this but do not have the leakage-free property.

**Transient handling**:

After the multisine starts, the mechanical system takes approximately one settling time (≈ 5τ, where τ is the dominant time constant) to reach periodic steady state. The first period(s) of data are contaminated by this transient and must not be used for training.

> Number of periods to discard ≥ ceil(5τ / T_ms)

For this system, the dominant time constant is set by the slowest pole. The rotational mode at ~5 Hz with damping ratio ζ has τ ≈ 1/(ζ·ω_res) — this depends on cb_eff and must be estimated from nominal parameters. A conservative estimate is τ ≈ 0.5 s → discard the first period if T_ms > 0.5 s, or the first 2–3 periods if T_ms = 0.2 s.

**Practical consequence for trajectory design**: the trajectory must be long enough to contain at least (n_discard + n_useful) periods. This sets a minimum trajectory duration that may exceed what the reference trajectory motion itself requires. Pad with a hold phase at the final position if needed.

**Implemented in `export_param_recovery.m`**: when `USE_MULTISINE=true`, the final hold is padded so the record contains an integer number of 1-second multisine periods. This gives `Δf = 1 Hz`, keeps all excited lines on DFT bins, and guarantees at least two full periods. Transient exclusion for training is still handled downstream by segment selection.

---

### 6.6 Requirement 2 — Frequency grid (harmonic, odd-only, resolution)

**Harmonic grid**: all excited frequencies are integer multiples of f0 = fs/N. This is required for the leakage-free property (Section 6.5). Use a harmonic grid.

**Frequency resolution**: the implementation uses a 1-second period, so `f0 = 1 Hz` at `fs = 20 kHz`. All trajectories are padded to an integer number of 1-second periods when multisine mode is enabled.

**Odd-only lines**: in a system identification context, using only odd-frequency multiples of f0 (i.e., excited at f0, 3f0, 5f0, ...) leaves even multiples empty. This has two benefits:
1. Nonlinear distortions of even order (quadratic terms) appear at even harmonics — they can be detected as energy at even lines, which serve as a nonlinearity check.
2. The noise floor can be estimated from the even lines without a separate noise measurement.

**Decision**: use odd-only harmonic lines within [f_low, f_high]. The `generate_multisine` implementation should:

```matlab
    f0    = 1;                               % frequency resolution, 1 s period
k_all = round(f_low/f0) : round(f_high/f0);   % harmonic indices in band
k_odd = k_all(mod(k_all, 2) == 1);      % odd harmonics only
F     = length(k_odd);                   % number of excited lines
freqs = k_odd * f0;                      % Hz
```

If `f_low/f0` is not an integer, round to the nearest harmonic — never interpolate. The number of lines F determines the Schroeder phase formula in Section 6.7. With odd-only lines, the 1-20 Hz band gives 10 excited lines, so `PE order = 2F = 20`, which is above the 13-parameter requirement.

### 6.7 Requirement 3 — Phase design (Schroeder phases)

A random phase multisine has crest factor (peak/RMS) ≈ √(2 ln F), which for F = 20 lines is ~3.5. This wastes force headroom — the peak amplitude is 3.5× the RMS, so to stay within saturation limits the RMS must be kept at 1/3.5 of the maximum.

**Schroeder phases** minimise the crest factor to approximately 1.5 regardless of F:

> φ_k = −k(k−1)π/F   for k = 1, ..., F

where k indexes the excited harmonics in order. The multisine signal is:

> f(n) = A · Σ_{k=1}^{F} cos(2π·f_k·n/fs + φ_k)

where A is chosen to achieve the target RMS after normalisation.

**Validation**: after generating the signal, compute `crest_factor = max(abs(f)) / rms(f)` and verify it is ≤ 2.0. If above 2.0, apply a random phase perturbation to each Schroeder phase (|δφ| ≤ 0.3 rad) and iterate until the crest factor is minimised — the Schroeder formula is a good starting point but local optimisation can improve it further. Do not iterate more than 50 times; the Schroeder value is already adequate.

**Implementation**: `export_param_recovery.m` uses the lecture convention `φ_k = -k(k-1)π/F` and deterministic mode-dependent time shifts so the active modes are not identical while preserving low crest factor.

---

### 6.8 Requirement 4 — Amplitude spectrum shaping

**Flat spectrum (default)**: all F excited lines get equal amplitude A. This is appropriate for broadband identification where no specific frequency is prioritised. Use flat spectrum for the (F1+F2) channel (translational X, no resonance) and the Fy channel (Y-axis, no resonance in the feedforward path).

**Shaped spectrum (resonance-targeted)**: for the (F1−F2) channel on T4, T7, T8, concentrate power near the rotational resonance (~5 Hz). A possible shaping:
- Lines below 3 Hz: half amplitude (less sensitive, rotational response dominated by static stiffness)
- Lines 3–8 Hz (near resonance): full amplitude
- Lines above 8 Hz: quarter amplitude (above resonance, rotational response rolls off as 1/ω²)

This concentrates power where the kb/Jb separation is most sensitive, improving the gradient signal for rotational parameters without increasing peak force.

**Implementation note**: shaping is applied before Schroeder phase computation. After shaping, renormalise the entire signal to the target amp_rms.

**Decision**: flat spectrum is implemented first. Measure which parameters converge slowest in the first training runs, then consider adding shaped spectrum for (F1−F2) if kb/Jb/Jh remain poorly identified.

### 6.9 Requirement 5 — MIMO channel design

The (N×3) output of `generate_multisine` contains three channels: [F_X1, F_X2, F_Y]. These must be designed so the three channels are distinguishable in the frequency domain — otherwise, a cross-coupling term in the model cannot be identified because it is impossible to attribute a response to a specific input.

**Two standard approaches:**

**Option A — Independent phase seeds**: all three channels excited at the same frequencies but with different random phase offsets added to the Schroeder phases. The signals are uncorrelated but not orthogonal in any single window. Works well for BPTT training because each trajectory window contains all channels simultaneously.

**Option B — Orthogonal frequency subsets**: split the harmonic lines into three non-overlapping subsets, one per channel. Channel 1 gets lines {1, 4, 7, ...}, channel 2 gets {2, 5, 8, ...}, channel 3 gets {3, 6, 9, ...}. The channels are perfectly orthogonal — each frequency is excited by exactly one channel. This allows exact FRF estimation per channel.

**Implementation decision**: for BPTT parameter recovery, `export_param_recovery.m` uses simultaneous mode-shaped multisines rather than strict orthogonal FRF experiments. Active physical modes are `common`, `diff`, and `y`; after mode summation, each active actuator channel is normalised to the requested RMS. For future hardware FRF estimation, strict orthogonal multisine experiments are still preferred.

**MIMO physical channels**: note that F_X1 and F_X2 map to symmetric (F1+F2) and anti-symmetric (F1−F2) directions. The implementation generates F_X1 and F_X2 directly; the model applies them to the corresponding actuators. The (F1+F2) and (F1−F2) decomposition is implicit in the model equations.

**Channel suppression by trajectory**: which channels carry non-zero energy is controlled by the trajectory's `ms_f_low` and `ms_f_high`. For a Y-only trajectory (T1, T6), set `ms_f_high` for Fy but set F_X1 and F_X2 to zero. Implementation: after generating the full (N×3) array using Option A, zero out channels that are not designated for this trajectory.

### 6.10 Requirement 6 — Amplitude normalisation

After phase assignment and shaping, normalise each channel independently so that:

> rms(f_channel) = amp_rms   [N]

where `amp_rms` is the target passed to `generate_multisine`. This is done by computing the current RMS and scaling the entire channel:

```matlab
for ch = 1:3
    current_rms = rms(f(:, ch));
    if current_rms > 0
        f(:, ch) = f(:, ch) * (amp_rms / current_rms);
    end
end
```

Note: if a channel carries zero energy for this trajectory (suppressed per Section 6.9), its RMS is zero — skip normalisation for that channel and leave it as zeros.

**Units check**: `amp_rms` is in Newtons. The Simulink model feedforward input must accept Newtons. Verify the unit convention with the model's actuator force scaling before finalising.

## 7. Sampling Strategy Change

**Current**: `TRAIN_SEGMENTS_PER_EPOCH = 8`, segments sampled randomly across the 6 trajectories. Some trajectories (especially T4) may not be sampled in a given epoch.

**Problem**: Random sampling means rotational parameters (kb, cb, Jb, Jh) receive gradient only when T4 is sampled. With 6 trajectories and 8 segments per epoch, T4 has expected coverage of 8/6 ≈ 1.3 segments/epoch but with high variance — it can be missed entirely in some epochs.

**Change**: With 8 trajectories and 8 segments per epoch, sample **exactly one segment from each trajectory per epoch**. This guarantees:
- Every parameter receives non-zero gradient every epoch
- T4, T7, T8 (rotational and all-parameters trajectories) contribute every epoch
- The 8 segments give balanced Fisher information coverage per epoch rather than in expectation only

**Implementation**: Replace the random segment sampler in `train_param_recovery.py` with a round-robin or fixed-one-per-trajectory sampler.

## 8. Force Logging (Future Consideration)

The LFR model is a plant model: it takes forces (F1, F2, Fy) as inputs and produces positions (X1, X2, Y) as outputs. Currently, the forces used as model inputs are the commanded/simulated forces from the Simulink controller output.

In the real hardware system, actual forces can be estimated from motor currents via the motor force constant. Logging actual forces and using them directly as model inputs (rather than commanded forces) would improve identification because:
- Actual forces may differ from commanded forces due to amplifier dynamics, saturation, and current control bandwidth
- Force measurements provide additional output channels: fitting F1+F2 and F1−F2 as outputs alongside positions dramatically improves the Fisher information condition number
- F1−F2 asymmetry during Y acceleration directly gives mh·d·Ÿ — enabling d identification by García's algebraic method in addition to the gradient-based approach

This is not required for the simulation-based parameter recovery but should be considered for real hardware identification.

## 9. Future: Jacobian-Based Trajectory Validation (Optimal Experiment Design)

Before committing to hardware experiments, the trajectory set should be validated using the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM):

$$F = \sum_{i=1}^{8} J_i^\top J_i, \quad J_i = \frac{\partial \hat{y}_i}{\partial \theta}$$

where $\hat{y}_i$ is the simulated position output for trajectory $i$ and $\theta$ is the full parameter vector.

**What this tells you:**
- **Rank of F**: must equal the number of trainable parameters (13 or 14 with d). Rank deficiency means at least one parameter is structurally unidentifiable from the trajectory set.
- **Eigenvalues of F**: small eigenvalues indicate poorly identified parameter directions (collinear gradients). The eigenvectors identify which parameter combinations are confounded.
- **Condition number**: large condition number means some parameters are identified orders of magnitude worse than others — a poorly designed trajectory set.

**Criterion**: E-optimality — maximise the minimum eigenvalue λ_min(F). This ensures the worst-identified parameter direction is as informative as possible, which is the right criterion when all 13/14 parameters must be recovered.

**Practical implementation**: PyTorch autograd already computes the Jacobian through the BPTT computation graph. Use `torch.autograd.functional.jacobian` on the simulate function evaluated at the true parameter values. Build F and check its spectrum. If λ_min is near zero, identify the corresponding eigenvector to find the confounded parameter pair, then design a trajectory that breaks that specific collinearity.

**Greedy trajectory selection**: Generate a large candidate pool of trajectories (varying Y positions, amplitudes, mode combinations). Select trajectories one at a time by choosing the candidate that maximally increases λ_min(F) at each step. Stop when the condition number is acceptable or improvement saturates.
